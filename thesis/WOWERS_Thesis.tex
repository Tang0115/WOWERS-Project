\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Municipal wastewater treatment in the United States consumes roughly 30.2~TWh of
electricity each year, and every publicly owned treatment works discharges treated
effluent through an outfall that runs continuously, at a flow already metered
for permit compliance, and often over a usable elevation drop. That hydraulic
energy is discarded. No prior assessment has quantified how much of it is recoverable
plant by plant, because the cost of searching seventeen thousand plants has exceeded
the value of the answer: only a small fraction are economic, and which fraction cannot
be known without examining all of them.

We propose the creation of a company, WOWERS, and present a proof-of-concept
infrastructure-intelligence platform on which a later minimum viable product
(MVP) can build. It screens the national fleet from public regulatory data
alone, ranks every plant on recoverable energy and project economics, and reports the
result as a calibrated band rather than a point estimate. It is a screening instrument,
not a validated predictor.

Four phases do the work. Phase~1 ingests approximately 279 million discharge
monitoring records from the Environmental Protection Agency's ICIS-NPDES system and
derives a flow duration curve per plant. Phase~2 converts each curve into an
annual-energy distribution by Monte-Carlo sampling. Phase~3 estimates net head from the
U.S.\ Geological Survey 3D Elevation Program under a plausibility gate and matches a
turbine from a vendor catalogue. Phase~4 builds a thirty-year financial scorecard and
calibrates the energy against measured capacity factors. A byte-deterministic
59-property GeoJSON export feeds a static React and MapLibre dashboard.

The results separate simulated from measured. Simulated: 17{,}148 plants screened yield
1{,}138 project-viable sites carrying 58.5891~MW and 409.17~GWh/yr at the physics
ceiling, against \$211.33M of capital cost, \$310.13M of net present value, and a
9.83~yr median payback. Measured: capacity factors from 629 river-hydropower plants over
9{,}798 plant-years and 115 metered conduit plants bound that ceiling to a band of
89.8--281~GWh/yr, floored on the only metered treated-wastewater conduit plant in the
country at a capacity factor of 0.1914. Instrumented installations are reported in the
international literature, but no metered wastewater-outfall generation
appears in the U.S.\ public generation datasets this calibration draws on, so every
energy figure here is modeled; an attempt to learn a correction ended when a national
label search returned 11 usable sites against a pre-set requirement of 50.

The platform makes a site visit targetable: 129 sites above 100~kW carry 81.3~\% of
portfolio value, collapsing a national search into a regional campaign. Three
improvements would close the remaining gaps: surveyed head in place of the
elevation-model proxy, an instrumented pilot with revenue-grade metering, and
delivery-layer work to carry the interface beyond demonstration scale.

\chapter{Introduction}

Municipal wastewater treatment is one of the largest electricity loads a local
government controls. The publicly owned treatment works of the United States draw
on the order of 30.2~TWh/yr, close to 0.8~\% of national electricity
consumption~\cite{epri2013}, and for many municipalities the treatment plant is
the single largest line on the utility bill. Those same plants discharge treated
effluent continuously, day and night, through outfalls that drop several meters
into a receiving river or estuary. The energy in that drop is real, it is
recoverable with machines that already exist, and at almost every plant in the
country it is currently dissipated as turbulence and heat. Recovering even a
fraction of it would turn a small part of a municipality's largest electricity
bill back into generation, using an asset the municipality already owns and
already operates around the clock.

The reason this has not happened at scale is not that the turbines are missing.
In-conduit machines suited to low head and continuous flow are commercially
available from several vendors, and a small number of installations have operated
for years. The obstacle is that nobody knows which plants are worth approaching.
A conventional feasibility study is commissioned one plant at a time and has to be
paid for before anyone knows whether that plant has usable head at all, so its
cost falls on the sites that turn out not to be worth building as heavily as on
the sites that do. At the scale this fleet operates that cost is not incidental:
the median viable site in the screen reported in Chapter~5 is a 12.96~kW machine
with an all-in capital cost of \$66{,}421, so any per-site engineering assessment
is a material fraction of the project it is assessing. With more than seventeen
thousand permitted treatment works in the country, a process that spends real
money per site to answer a yes-or-no question cannot be run across the fleet. The
constraint is not turbine technology or capital availability. It is the cost of
finding out, and it is a screening problem rather than an engineering one.

We propose the creation of a company, WOWERS (the Waste Outfall Water Energy
Recovery System), to solve that screening problem as an
infrastructure-intelligence service: a platform that identifies, ranks, and bounds
micro-hydropower recovery opportunities at wastewater outfalls nationally, from
public data, before anyone visits a site. The proposition is that the first
pass of a feasibility study (how much flow, how much head, what machine, what
it would cost, what it would return) can be performed from regulatory records
that every plant in the country already files, at a marginal cost per site of
approximately zero, and that the resulting ranked shortlist is what a developer, a
municipality, or a state energy office actually needs to spend
engineering money in the right place.

In its end state the service runs as a continuous pipeline. Public discharge
records are ingested as they are published and converted into a flow signal for
every permitted plant; a national elevation model supplies the drop between each
plant and its permitted outfall; a physical model converts flow and head into an
energy estimate with an explicit uncertainty band; a turbine catalogue selects the
machine that suits each site's operating point; a cost and financial model turns
that machine into capital cost, revenue, payback, and return; and the whole
national result is published to an interactive map where a user filters to a
state, a size class, or an economic band and exports the shortlist. A customer
arrives with a question (which plants in our state are worth a feasibility
study) and leaves with a ranked, priced, and bounded answer in minutes rather
than months.

The development required to reach that end state is substantial, and it divides
into two tracks. The technical development covers data acquisition and cleaning
from federal regulatory sources, the physical and financial modeling chain, the
calibration of that chain against real operating plants, the serving layer that
publishes results, and the interface a customer uses. The logistical development
covers everything the technology does not touch: customer discovery with
municipalities and developers, the regulatory pathway through FERC conduit
exemptions, relationships with turbine manufacturers and installers, financing
structures for municipal customers who cannot carry capital cost, and the field
validation program that would eventually replace modeled head with measured
head. This work will discuss the business model of WOWERS with additional focus on
the first steps in its technical development.

\section{Thesis Statement}

We propose that a national screen for micro-hydropower recovery at wastewater
outfalls can be built entirely from public regulatory data, and that its output is
commercially useful provided its uncertainty is stated rather than hidden. The
business discussion covers the problem, the service, the target market, the
competitive landscape, pricing, distribution, market size, and the growth path for
WOWERS as a venture. The proof-of-concept develops the technical half of that
proposition: a four-phase pipeline that screens 17{,}148 treatment works, models
energy and project economics for those that survive, calibrates the energy result
against measured capacity factors from real operating plants, and publishes the
outcome through a static data contract to an interactive national map.

\section{Thesis Outline}

Chapter~2 reviews the background: where outfall energy is lost, the six
limitations that keep recovery from scaling, what current practice already does
well, a first-principles primer on hydropower screening from public data, and the
prior national assessments this work sits beside. Chapter~3 presents the business
model for WOWERS across problem, service, target market, competition, pricing,
distribution, market potential, and growth. Chapter~4 is the design chapter and
the bulk of the work: the system overview, the acquisition of regulatory and
elevation data, the four-phase processing pipeline from flow features through
Monte-Carlo energy, head estimation, turbine selection and project economics, the
calibration and validation work including the machine-learning path that was
abandoned, the export layer, and the visualization system. Chapter~5 reports what
the platform produced: best-case and calibrated performance, the exclusion
funnel and its defense, energy and financial results, the capacity-factor
calibration, the machine-learning negative result, frontend performance, and the
end-to-end integration test. Chapter~6 concludes and names the work that would
carry the platform from a screen to a validated product. Three appendices carry
the data dictionary and funnel tables, the calibration and sensitivity captures,
and the Monte-Carlo distributions.

\chapter{Background and Prior Work}

Municipal wastewater treatment is an energy-intensive public service. Nationally,
U.S.\ publicly owned treatment works (POTWs) consume on the order of 30.2 TWh/yr of
electricity, about 0.8\% of total U.S.\ electricity use in the reference year of the
Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) sector study~\cite{epri2013}. At the same time,
treated effluent leaves most plants through gravity or pressurized outfalls that drop
several meters of unused hydraulic head into receiving waters. That residual head is
dissipated as turbulence and heat rather than converted to useful work. Micro-hydropower
recovery at the outfall is therefore an energy-\emph{recovery} opportunity nested inside
an energy-\emph{consuming} facility: the plant already pays for aeration, pumping, and
solids handling, while a fraction of the kinetic and potential energy in the discharge
stream goes unused.

The physical opportunity is real, but capture has been rare. There are six common
limitations that keep outfall energy recovery from scaling across the U.S.\ POTW fleet.
These limitations will now be discussed in more detail.

\section{Wastewater Outfall Energy Loss and Recovery Limitations}

The six limitations that dominate practice are (1)~unmeasured site head, (2)~incomplete
or corrupted flow records, (3)~misapplied river-hydro capacity factors, (4)~debris and
maintenance downtime that physics models omit, (5)~project-by-project rather than
fleet-scale screening, and (6)~opaque unit economics relative to plant power bills.
Each limitation is treated below in that order.

Unmeasured hydraulic head is the first barrier. Gross head at an outfall is the elevation
difference between the plant hydraulic grade line and the receiving-water surface, less
friction and minor losses in the conduit. Unlike river dams, WWTP outfalls are not
instrumented with published stage records, and national permit databases carry facility
coordinates far more reliably than outfall invert elevations. Screening studies that
substitute literature archetype heads (for example triangular distributions of a few
meters for small plants) can mis-rank sites by a factor of two or more when real
topographic relief differs from the archetype~\cite{ornl2014}. Rule of thumb in the field
is that net heads below about 1--2~m rarely justify a conventional reaction turbine
without a very large continuous flow.

Incomplete or corrupted discharge records form the second barrier. Flow is reported to
EPA through Discharge Monitoring Reports (DMRs) under ICIS-NPDES, but the national archive
contains sentinel values (for example design-flow entries of 999.0~MGD), unit slips between
gallons per day and million gallons per day, and primary-outfall selection errors that
prefer a single corrupted row over a consistently reported treatment outfall. After
cleaning, the WOWERS Phase~1 cohort retains 17,148 active POTWs with usable mean flow; the
pre-clean archive required extensive guards precisely because uncorrected DMR rows
propagate into energy estimates that look precise and are wrong~\cite{epa_echo}.

Capacity-factor misapplication is the third barrier. Run-of-river small hydro in the DOE
HydroSource Existing Hydropower Assets (EHA) dataset shows median annual capacity factors
near 0.39 for plants in the 0.1--5~MW class (629 plants, 9,798 plant-years) because of
seasonal drought, peak sizing, and environmental-flow bypasses~\cite{hydrosource_eha}.
WWTP outfalls track municipal demand, typically varying only $\pm$15--25\% seasonally, and
have no ecological minimum-flow reservation on the effluent itself. Applying the river
median CF to a continuous municipal conduit therefore understates recoverable energy by
roughly a factor of two relative to a WWTP-appropriate CF near 0.60, the
optimistic upper tier we adopt after anchoring to the Portland Conduit~3 in-pipe
installation~\cite{ferc_p14498, lucidpipe}. That anchor is a design projection
rather than a measurement, and Section~4.4.1 sets it against the metered conduit
plants that sit well below it.

Debris fouling, minimum-flow cutoffs, and maintenance downtime constitute the fourth
limitation. Physics ceilings that integrate $\eta\rho g Q H$ over a flow-duration curve
with high availability (implied CF near 0.87 in the WOWERS Phase~2 P50 ceiling of
409.2~GWh/yr) omit screens clogging, grit abrasion in wastewater, and planned outages.
The best-documented continuous-conduit anchor, the Portland Conduit~3 in-pipe
installation, carries a design capacity factor between 0.628 and 0.806 depending on which
filed and as-built figures are paired, and every value in that range sits below a pure
physics ceiling~\cite{ferc_p14498, lucidpipe}. Expecting
fleet-wide CF above about 0.65 without site-specific O\&M modeling is not supported by
published installs.

The fifth limitation is institutional rather than hydraulic: recovery projects are
originated one municipality at a time. Vendors such as LucidEnergy, Rentricity, and CINK
Hydro-Energy publish case sheets for individual pressurized mains or WWTP references, but
no public national inventory ranks all U.S.\ POTW outfalls by recoverable energy and
payback under a shared method~\cite{rentricity,cink}. Without a fleet screen, capital
flows to the plants that already have a consultant, not necessarily to the plants with the
best physics.

The sixth limitation is economic opacity at plant scale. EPRI reports national wastewater
electricity use of 30.2~TWh/yr and observed energy intensities from about 3,300~kWh/MG for
plants under 2~MGD down to about 1,600~kWh/MG for plants above 100~MGD~\cite{epri2013}.
Against that bill, a micro-turbine that offsets a median of roughly 1.1\% of plant
consumption across the scored fleet (Phase~4 screening sanity check) looks trivial in
isolation even when the
portfolio NPV is large. Operators therefore under-invest unless screening shows named
payback, CapEx, and turbine match rather than a generic ``green energy'' claim.
These common limitations will now be set against what current practice already achieves.

\section{Current Practice and In-Conduit Recovery Techniques}

In-conduit and effluent hydropower is a proven niche, not an empty category. Three
commercial lines illustrate what practitioners already do well, which of the six
limitations they address, and which they leave open.

LucidEnergy's LucidPipe system places spherical in-pipe turbines in large-diameter
pressurized transmission mains (approximately 24--96~in). The flagship installation is the
Conduit~3 Hydroelectric Project in Portland, Oregon, developed with the Portland Water
Bureau under Federal Energy Regulatory Commission docket P-14498. The 2013 application
describes four customized pipe sections, each holding a sealed vertical turbine unit, at a
total nameplate capacity of 170~kW and an estimated 1,200~MWh/yr~\cite{ferc_p14498}, while
the as-built system is reported at 200~kW across four 42~in machines and an expected
1,100~MWh/yr~\cite{lucidpipe}. Both energy figures are design estimates rather than metered
output: the plant sits below the 1~MW threshold at which generators file Form
EIA-923~\cite{eia923}, so no public annual generation record exists for it. Pairing the
filed and as-built figures gives an implied capacity factor between
$1{,}100{,}000/(200\times 8{,}760)=0.628$ and $1{,}200{,}000/(170\times 8{,}760)=0.806$.
That project demonstrates that continuous municipal-conduit duty is designed and financed
at capacity factors well above river-hydro medians, and it addresses limitation~(3) in an
operating installation, though on projected rather than metered generation. It does not catch limitations~(1),~(5), or~(6) for the national WWTP fleet: head and
flow are known because the utility already operates the main, and the project was
originated as a single utility partnership rather than from a ranked national list. The
hydraulic regime is also drinking water rather than wastewater, so debris and corrosion
risks are milder than at a secondary-effluent outfall.

Rentricity supplies reverse-pump (Francis-type) machines certified to NSF~61/372 for
potable and wastewater service. Public featured projects include approximately 32~kW at
2.4~MGD and 40~psi, and approximately 360~kW at 2--12~MGD with 175--250~ft of head
~\cite{rentricity}. These installs show that wastewater-certified hardware exists in the
tens-to-hundreds-of-kilowatts band relevant to medium POTWs, addressing part of
limitation~(4) through productized O\&M expectations. Annual generation (hence CF) is not
published per site, so Rentricity cannot yet anchor a national calibration band the way
LucidPipe can. Screening still depends on the utility bringing a known pressure and flow
to the vendor.

CINK Hydro-Energy manufactures crossflow, Kaplan, Pelton, and Francis machines and
explicitly lists wastewater treatment plants among use cases. The company reports more than
450 turbines in more than 50 countries and advertises operation from 6\% to 100\% of design
flow~\cite{cink}. That part-load envelope is well matched to flat municipal flow-duration
curves and therefore softens limitation~(4) relative to peak-sized river machines. As with
Rentricity, published per-site annual kWh figures suitable for CF calibration are sparse,
and project origination remains site-solicited rather than fleet-ranked.

Complementary U.S.\ vendors (Canyon Hydro conduit installations, Emrgy canal modules,
Turbulent vortex units, Natel fish-safe Kaplan variants) enlarge the hardware menu but do
not change the pattern: hardware and site engineering exist; a reproducible national
prioritization of POTW outfalls does not~\cite{doe_vision}. Current practice therefore
catches local hydraulic feasibility and, in the LucidPipe case, continuous-conduit CF. It
misses national ranking under shared assumptions, systematic treatment of DMR data quality,
and calibrated portfolio energy bands.

The peer-reviewed literature supplies what the vendor record does not: instrumented
measurement. Chae \emph{et al.} installed a flow-variable semi-Kaplan micro-hydropower
unit on the effluent of an operating municipal treatment plant in South Korea and monitored
it for more than a year, reporting 68.1~MWh of annual generation under severe flow
fluctuation~\cite{chae2015}. Power \emph{et al.} examined turbine selection and design
optimization across four Irish treatment plants, and found that flow variability from storm
events and demand change materially shifts the optimum machine~\cite{power2017}. Two
consequences follow for this work. First, the claim this thesis makes about missing ground
truth must be stated precisely: measured wastewater-outfall generation does exist in the
literature, as individual instrumented case studies, and what is absent is a machine-readable
national record pairing head, flow, and metered annual energy across enough sites to
calibrate or train against: the specific gap that ends the attempt of Section~4.4.2.
Second, the flow-variability finding independently supports the part-load emphasis of the
turbine-selection rules in Section~4.3.5: a machine chosen at peak flow is the wrong machine
for a duty curve as flat as a municipal outfall's.

A public-data screening primer that makes those gaps precise will now be discussed.

\section{Public-Data Screening of Hydropower Potential (Primer)}

This section discusses only the \emph{screening} problem (estimating which plants merit
a site visit), not detailed civil design, interconnection studies, or turbine manufacturing.
The enabling physics is the standard hydropower relation
\begin{equation}
  P = \eta\,\rho\,g\,Q\,H,
  \label{eq:hydro}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is electrical power, $\eta$ is overall efficiency, $\rho$ is water density,
$g$ is gravitational acceleration, $Q$ is volumetric flow, and $H$ is net head. Annual
energy follows by integrating power over time, or equivalently by integrating
Equation~\eqref{eq:hydro} along a flow-duration curve (FDC) with an availability factor.
For municipal outfalls, $Q$ is observed (DMR monthly averages), while $H$ must be inferred
from topography or plant drawings.

Three public data layers make a national screen possible in principle. First, EPA ECHO /
ICIS-NPDES identifies active POTW permits and publishes facility attributes used to build
the candidate set~\cite{epa_echo}. Second, DMR parameter 50050 (flow) supplies the time
series from which mean flow, percentiles, and a 20-point FDC are computed; the WOWERS
ingest processes on the order of 279~million DMR rows spanning fiscal years 2009--2024.
Third, the USGS 3DEP elevation service supplies point elevations that, differenced between
plant and outfall coordinates and reduced by a head-loss fraction, yield a DEM-proxy net
head in place of literature archetype triangles~\cite{usgs_3dep}. Ground-truth generation
and capacity-factor benchmarks additionally draw on EIA/EHA HydroSource tables for existing
hydropower assets~\cite{hydrosource_eha}.

A defensible screen must still separate physics ceilings from calibrated expectations.
Substituting median river-hydro CF for continuous effluent over-haircuts energy; omitting
debris and downtime overstates it. The WOWERS response, developed in Chapters~4 and~5, is
to report a physics ceiling (409.2~GWh/yr at the P2-SEED baseline, the site-keyed
Monte-Carlo re-baseline of Section~4.3.3, whose numbering every figure in this thesis
uses) beside a calibrated band
of 89.8--281~GWh/yr, floored on the only metered treated-wastewater conduit plant in the
country and bounded above by the Portland Conduit~3 design
projection~\cite{hydrosource_eha,lucidpipe}. Prior national
methods that stop at resource potential, or that calibrate only against river fleets,
therefore leave a methodological gap that Section~2.4 compares explicitly. Why those prior
methods remain limited for POTW outfall recovery will now be discussed.

\section{Prior National Assessments and Calibration Approaches}

National hydropower assessment literature is rich for rivers and canals and thin for
WWTP outfalls; thin, but not empty, and the closest prior work is European rather than
American. M\'erida Garc\'ia \emph{et al.} screened Spain's municipal and industrial
wastewater discharges for micro-hydropower potential using discharge licenses as the flow
source~\cite{merida2021}, which is the same methodological move this work makes with
ICIS-NPDES discharge monitoring reports and is the nearest published analogue to the WOWERS
approach. The same group extended the method across several European Union member states,
covering drinking, pressurized irrigation, and wastewater networks~\cite{mcnabola2021}, and
a comparable national assessment has been reported for Lithuania~\cite{punys2022}. A related
review draws operational lessons from installed wastewater-system
hydropower~\cite{novara2021}. None of these covers the United States, none reports a
per-plant ranked list of the kind Chapter~5 produces, and, most relevant to Chapter~4,
none calibrates its energy estimate against metered generation from the same asset class,
which is the specific contribution Section~4.4.1 claims. The comparison that follows uses a
single common metric, reported or implied annual energy (or capacity factor) at national or
multi-state scale, across at least three named prior lines of work and the WOWERS screen. The scale and method contrast
can be seen in Table~\ref{tab:prior_work}.

DOE's \emph{Hydropower Vision} and the related Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) New
Stream-reach Development cost work supply the sector framing and the power-law CapEx form
used throughout small-hydro screening, but they do not inventory WWTP effluent
drops~\cite{doe_vision,ornl2014}. Their value to WOWERS is methodological (cost curves,
turbine envelopes), not a POTW energy total that can be compared one-to-one with
409.2~GWh/yr.

The HydroSource EHA annual capacity-factor workbook is the strongest empirical
calibration source available without site visits. Restricting to the 0.1--5~MW class yields
629 plants and 9,798 plant-years with median CF 0.390 and 25th/75th percentiles 0.254 /
0.541~\cite{hydrosource_eha}. Those statistics correctly bound a \emph{conservative
floor} for any small-hydro claim. They incorrectly describe WWTP duty cycles if used as a
central estimate, because seasonal river drivers listed in Section~2.1 do not apply to
municipal effluent. The WOWERS calibration therefore retains the EHA range (119--194~GWh/yr
depending on percentile and MW sub-bucket) as corroboration rather than as the floor itself,
places the reported floor on metered treated-wastewater generation at 89.8~GWh/yr, and adds
an optimistic tier at CF~0.60 (281~GWh/yr), set below the 0.628--0.806 range implied by the Portland Conduit~3
filed and as-built figures~\cite{ferc_p14498, lucidpipe}. Because those figures are
projections rather than metered output, the same section benchmarks them against the 115
EHA Canal/Conduit plants that do report measured generation to
EIA-923~\cite{hydrosource_eha, eia923}, whose median capacity factor of 0.2439
corroborates the floor and undercuts the upper tier.

ORNL conduit and canal work, including municipal conduit install-cost samples and the
baseload cost model (BCM) canal/conduit installed-capital-cost equation, improves CapEx
realism for in-conduit machines but again estimates \emph{engineering potential} or
\$/kW rather than a cleaned national POTW energy band tied to DMR
FDCs~\cite{ornl2014,osti3002705}. FERC's qualifying-conduit pathway under the Hydropower
Regulatory Efficiency Act of 2013 further lowers soft costs for man-made municipal conduits
(Notice of Intent rather than a full license for facilities $\leq$40~MW), which improves
project economics once a site is chosen but does not rank sites~\cite{ferc_conduit}.

Plant energy-\emph{consumption} literature (EPRI Table~5-1 observed kWh/MG by flow band;
WEF Manual of Practice~32 curves retained for sensitivity) closes the offset narrative:
national wastewater use near 30.2~TWh/yr and validated plant-level intensities give context
for why an 89.8--281~GWh/yr recovery band is simultaneously material at portfolio scale and
small as a fraction of treatment load~\cite{epri2013,wef_mop32}. ENERGY STAR and EPA
planning guides corroborate the order of magnitude without replacing the EPRI observed
grid~\cite{epa2013}.

Taken together, prior assessments either (a)~quantify broad hydropower resource and cost,
(b)~measure CF on river fleets, or (c)~document individual conduit installs. None combine a
17,000-plant POTW filter, DMR-derived FDCs, DEM-proxy heads, vendor turbine matching, and
an explicit physics-versus-calibrated energy band. That combined screen is the technical
core developed in Chapter~4, and the quantitative gaps between ceiling and calibrated
expectation are reported in Chapter~5. The business model that motivates building such a
screen will now be discussed.

\chapter{Business Model}

Every figure in this chapter is an estimate produced by this work rather than an
observed commercial result, and the energy-derived figures carry the tier they rest
on. Where a number depends on the capacity-factor assumption of Section~4.4.1, both
ends are given: the physics ceiling that the model computes and the measured floor
that the evidence supports. No customer has yet paid for any of the services
described here, and Section~3.8 says what would have to happen before the revenue
arithmetic of Section~3.7 could be called a forecast rather than a plan.

\section{The Problem}

Municipal wastewater treatment consumes roughly 30.2~TWh/yr of electricity in the
United States, and energy is among the largest controllable lines in a treatment
plant's operating budget~\cite{epri2013,epa2013}. A fraction of that is
recoverable at the point where treated effluent is discharged: outfalls run
continuously, at flows their operators already meter for permit compliance, and
frequently over a usable elevation drop. This work screens 17,148 active publicly
owned treatment works and finds 1,138 where recovery clears a viability gate, of
which the 129 sites above 100~kW carry 81.3~\% of the portfolio net present value.

The value is real and it is almost impossible to find. Three complications explain
why it has stayed unexploited, and each is a specific obstacle rather than general
difficulty. First, the hit rate is 0.75~\%: 129 sites out of 17,148. Until
bulk regulatory data and cheap computation existed, the cost of searching exceeded
the value of what was found, because screening one plant by consultant costs
thousands of dollars and screening all of them now costs almost nothing. Second,
head is not measured anywhere at national scale. This work estimates it
from a digital elevation model with a plausibility gate, and 360 of the 1,138
viable sites still carry a literature-archetype head rather than an
elevation-derived one, so the single most important
physical input to the energy estimate is also its weakest. Third, no metered
wastewater generation record exists to calibrate against. A nationwide search of
Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and Department of Energy records returned
exactly one treated-wastewater conduit plant with measured output, and it has been
offline since 2018. The absence of records is itself the finding, and it is why this
chapter prices services rather than guaranteed energy.

How those three complications shape the service offered will now be discussed.

\section{The Solution}

We propose the creation of a company, WOWERS, to operate the national screening and
deal-origination layer that the search cost of Section~3.1 has so far prevented from
existing. The platform ingests approximately 279 million discharge monitoring
records covering every active treatment works in the country, estimates per-site
recoverable energy with a Monte-Carlo physics model, matches commercially available
turbines, scores each site on net present value, internal rate of return and
payback, and publishes the ranked result. The product is the answer to one question:
which of the seventeen thousand plants is worth a site visit.

We do not manufacture turbines, install them, own the assets, or take energy-yield
risk. Those roles are already occupied by competent firms, discussed in
Section~3.4, and each of them currently finds its own sites one at a time. The
deliverable per qualified site is a one-page technical sheet carrying exactly what a
manufacturer needs to quote: rated capacity, net head, design flow, a flow
duration curve summary, the recommended turbine class, the permitting tier, and the
discharge-permit contact already published in the Environmental Protection Agency's
compliance database~\cite{epa_echo}. The screening layer is the asset; the report
and the introduction are what is sold.

Deliberately excluded is any contract in which WOWERS is paid for delivered
kilowatt-hours. Section~3.5 explains why the calibration uncertainty documented in
Section~4.4.1 makes that an unpriceable risk at present.

\section{The Target Market}

The archetype customer is a mid-size municipal treatment works with in-house
engineering capacity, and the concrete example carried through this chapter is
Fall River Wastewater Treatment Plant, Massachusetts. The model rates it at
169.0~kW, with an estimated all-in capital cost of \$538k, estimated annual revenue
of \$179k/yr, and an estimated simple payback of 3.1~yr at the physics ceiling. Fall
River is not the largest opportunity in the portfolio; it is the best-qualified one,
because it clears 100~kW, reaches the high-confidence head tier, and sits in a
Northeast cluster where Lowell Regional Wastewater Utility, rated 124.9~kW, can be
visited on the same trip.

What makes such a plant hard to serve is not technical. It is that the same site
looks like a different investment depending on which energy assumption is applied,
and the honest assumption is the unflattering one. Re-scored at the measured floor
of Section~4.4.1, Fall River's payback moves from 3.1~yr to 16.8~yr, which no
municipal capital committee would approve. Applying a 50~\% capital subsidy at the
same floor returns it to 8.3~yr, which most would. The commercial consequence is
that a funding path is not a closing detail but part of the opening conversation.
Three federal programs make that path nameable rather than hypothetical: the Clean
Water State Revolving Fund, whose energy-conservation category explicitly includes
onsite renewable energy at treatment works and which lends below market with
principal forgiveness available in many states~\cite{epa_cwsrf_energy}; the
Department of Agriculture's Water and Waste Disposal program for communities of
10,000 or fewer, where needs-based grants reach 75~\%~\cite{usda_rd_wwd}; and
elective payment of the clean-electricity investment credit, treated in
Section~3.8. Notably, the Water Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act is
\emph{not} on that list: its minimum project size is \$20M, or \$5M for communities
of 25,000 or fewer~\cite{epa_wifia}, and the largest single site in this portfolio
carries \$3.7M of capital cost, so no individual project can reach it.

\section{Competition}

The firms most often named as competitors are better understood as distribution
channels. Lucid Energy built the in-conduit installation on a Portland water main
that anchors the optimistic tier of Section~4.4.1~\cite{ferc_p14498,lucidpipe};
Rentricity has an installed base in water and wastewater pressure
recovery~\cite{rentricity}; CINK Hydro-Energy supplies crossflow machines including
wastewater applications~\cite{cink}. Their strengths should be conceded plainly:
each has hardware in the ground, operating references, and engineering depth in
turbine design that this work does not have and does not attempt. What none of them
publishes is a national screen. Each identifies sites through its own sales effort,
one plant at a time, which is the search cost of Section~3.1 borne privately and
repeatedly. A ranked national list is therefore complementary to their business
rather than competitive with it, and Section~3.6 routes qualified sites to them by
recommended turbine class.

The real competition is of two kinds. The first is the energy service company
performing an in-house audit: firms of this type already sell efficiency work into
these plants, already hold the customer relationship, and could add outfall
recovery to their existing scope. They are the most credible threat to this
business, and the defense is narrow -- publishing the methodology early and signing
channel agreements before the capability is internalized. The second competitor, and
by a wide margin the larger, is inaction. A plant that has never been screened has no
reason to act, and 73.3~\% of the paper-viable population falls below 25~kW, where
the economics genuinely do not work. Anyone who examined the aggregate would have
seen thousands of uneconomic micro-projects and walked away rationally. The value
appears only after the whole population is ranked, which is the specific thing this
work did.

\section{Pricing Model}

Three tiers are proposed, and each carries the derivation it rests on rather than a
chosen number.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Screening report, \$8--15k per utility.} A custom run for one
    owner, with per-site detail, sensitivity bands and a methodology appendix.
    Derivation: approximately 1~\% of the estimated median net present value of a
    100--250~kW site, which the model puts near \$1.1M.
  \item \emph{Data subscription, \$300--900 per month.} Filtered access to the
    national database for manufacturers, energy service companies and consultants.
    Derivation: published cost-per-lead benchmarks for the manufacturing and
    industrial sector run roughly \$120--350 per lead, against a blended
    cross-industry figure near \$198~\cite{cpl_benchmark}. Thirty routed sites per
    year therefore carry \$3.6--10.5k/yr of lead-replacement value, and the
    subscription is priced across that same range. This tier was
    \emph{revised downward} during the citation pass described below.
  \item \emph{Origination fee, 2--4~\% of project capital cost, on financial
    close.} Derivation: the U.S.\ Treasury's position on renewable-energy developer
    fees, tested in \emph{California Ridge Wind Energy v.\ United States}, is that
    such fees should normally fall in the 3--5~\% range of project cost absent
    unusual circumstances; the Treasury reduced a claimed 12.3~\% fee to 4.8~\% in
    that case~\cite{nrf_devfees}. A developer earning that fee carries capital and
    development risk across the whole project. WOWERS carries neither: it originates
    and qualifies the site and does nothing else. Pricing at 2--4~\% therefore sits at
    or below the low end of what a full developer commands, which is the intended
    relationship. At the estimated median capital cost of \$552.1k for sites above
    100~kW this is \$11.0--22.1k per closed project.
  \item \emph{State or agency data layer, \$25--75k per engagement.} GIS exports, a
    white-label report and state-specific overlays. This tier's derivation remains
    unsourced: it was scaled to an assumed state energy-office procurement value and no
    public award record establishing that range was located. Two real award values
    would settle it.
\end{itemize}

One of these prices did not survive being sourced. The subscription tier was first
proposed at \$1--2k per month on an assumed \$3k per qualified lead; published
benchmarks put an industrial lead an order of magnitude lower, which supports
\$3.6--10.5k/yr of replacement value rather than the \$90k/yr the original derivation
implied. The price is revised accordingly. The counter-argument is not dismissed: a
WOWERS lead is an engineered site sheet, not a marketing contact, and may command
more, but that cannot be settled from published benchmarks, only from the customer
conversations named in Section~3.6.

Energy-as-a-service was considered and rejected for now. It would have WOWERS carry
yield risk against a calibration spanning a factor of 4.6, from the 409.2~GWh/yr
ceiling to the 89.8~GWh/yr the only metered treated-wastewater plant implies, with no
wastewater-specific ground truth in the datasets available here, the same scarcity
that ended the machine-learning attempt of Section~4.4.2. Guaranteeing generation
against an uncertainty this thesis states it cannot quantify would underwrite a risk we
have documented as unquantified. The position taken is fee-based revenue until a
metered pilot exists, after which a yield-linked contract can be priced on an
observed capacity factor rather than a substituted one.

\section{Marketing and Distribution}

Three stages are proposed in sequence, each with a single goal.

\emph{Stage one, publish and harvest inbound, months 0--6.} The screened dataset
and the methodology are released publicly and the dashboard of Section~4.6 is kept
live. The goal is credibility and inbound contact at near-zero cost: open data
generates citations, agency enquiries and manufacturer interest without a sales
function. The analysis service is gated; the data is not.

\emph{Stage two, channel agreements, months 3--12.} Qualified sites are routed to
the manufacturer whose product line matches the recommended machine, using the
turbine class the pipeline already assigns -- crossflow, Kaplan or Francis. The goal
is three signed channel partners, because a partner carries the eighteen-to-thirty-six
month municipal procurement cycle that a two-person company cannot.

\emph{Stage three, direct paid engagements, months 6--24.} Screening reports and
origination fees are sold against the cohort above 100~kW, either directly or jointly
with a channel partner. The goal is the first revenue that is not grant-funded.

The stages are ordered by cost, not by attractiveness. Stage three is where revenue
per engagement becomes meaningful, and it is placed last because it depends on
references that stages one and two produce. The sequence also has an unstarted
precondition: ten customer-validation conversations, with utility operations
directors, energy service company development leads, turbine manufacturers and state
energy offices. Until those occur, this section describes an intention rather than a
tested channel, and it is labeled as such.

\section{Market Potential}

The arithmetic is shown so that the size of this opportunity is not overstated, and
the first point is a distinction that is easy to blur. The \$310.1M portfolio net
present value reported in Chapter~5 accrues to \emph{plant owners}. It is not
revenue available to WOWERS, and treating it as a market would misstate the business
by two orders of magnitude.

The addressable market for a screening and origination service is the fee pool.
Taking the total addressable market as origination against the full viable
portfolio: 1,138 sites carrying \$211.3M of estimated capital cost, at a 3~\%
origination fee, gives \$6.3M of one-time fees, plus screening engagements. The
serviceable addressable market is the cohort where a fee is actually collectable,
namely the 129 sites above 100~kW carrying \$98.9M of capital cost, which at the same
3~\% gives \$3.0M. A realistic obtainable share of roughly 10~\% of that
serviceable market is \$0.3M of origination fees. Adding screening engagements and
channel subscriptions at the revised prices of Section~3.5 (three subscribers
across the \$300--900 per month band contribute \$11--32k/yr, not the
\$36--72k/yr the original \$1--2k price implied) puts five-year revenue near
\$0.4--1.0M.

Three qualifications matter more than the point estimate. The figures above rest on
the physics ceiling; at the measured floor with municipal financing and a 50~\%
subsidy the bankable cohort above 100~kW falls from 129 sites to 59, and the
serviceable market halves with it. The conversion assumptions behind the obtainable
share are unvalidated, for the reason given in Section~3.6. And the subscription
component moved downward when its derivation was sourced, which is a reminder that
the least-evidenced numbers in this chapter are the ones describing revenue to
WOWERS rather than value to plant owners: the pipeline outputs are reproducible,
the prices are not. What the arithmetic supports is a consulting and data business
for a small team, not a venture-scale company, and stating that plainly is more
useful than a larger number that the evidence in Chapter~5 would contradict.

\section{Future Vision and Project Timeline}

The first milestone to be built is not a product feature. It is an
instrumented pilot at Fall River Wastewater Treatment Plant, carrying revenue-grade
metering from commissioning. Its purpose is to convert the weakest link in this
entire thesis into data: one year of measured generation from a wastewater outfall
turbine would replace the substituted capacity factor of Section~4.4.1 with an
observed one, and would do more for the credibility of every number in this chapter
than any amount of additional screening. It is sequenced first for that reason.

The company's funding path is non-dilutive first and probably non-dilutive only. The
National Science Foundation's Small Business Innovation Research program awards up to
\$305k at Phase~I~\cite{nsf_sbir}, and a documented calibration band, a published
negative result and a reproducible test suite are the posture such programs reward. On
the customer side, elective payment lets tax-exempt and governmental owners take
clean-energy credits as cash~\cite{irs_elective_pay}, and the clean-electricity
investment credit was retained in full for hydropower through
2033~\cite{nha_obbba}. The threshold that matters for this
portfolio is 1~MW: facilities below it are exempt from the prevailing-wage and
apprenticeship conditions while still reaching the full 30~\% credit, and are
outside the domestic-content phaseout that otherwise reduces elective-pay
value~\cite{irs_pwa,irs_elective_pay,irs_notice_2024_84}. Of the 1,138 viable sites,
1,133 fall below 1~MW. The five that do not include both marquee-scale sites, which
therefore carry a compliance load the rest of the portfolio does not.

A second restriction does \emph{not} carry the sub-megawatt exemption, and it bears on
the equipment recommendation of Section~3.4. For construction beginning after
31~December 2025, the credit is denied if a project received ``material assistance from
a prohibited foreign entity,'' measured as the share of direct costs \emph{not} traceable
to such an entity. A project fails below a threshold that rises annually: 40~\% in 2026,
then 45, 50 and 55~\% through 2029, and 60~\% thereafter~\cite{usc7701}, with interim
computation safe harbours issued in February 2026~\cite{irs_notice_2026_15}. The 2026
bar is permissive (up to 60~\% of direct costs may come from restricted sources), so
a European turbine with mixed-origin components is unlikely to fail it today. It
tightens to 60~\% clean content by 2030, which turns supplier nationality from a
footnote into a procurement criterion, and it binds the small sites as much as the
large. This is the one tax question here that scale does not resolve.

After the pilot, the same pipeline extends to other continuously flowing
infrastructure -- pressure-reducing valves, industrial cooling discharge, irrigation
canal drops, mine dewatering -- each needing only a new ingest module. None of it is
built or sized here, and it is named only to keep the boundary of what has been
demonstrated visible. Chapter~6 states what was tested and what remains open.

\section{System Overview}                                % WP T2 / Joint

The WOWERS platform is a national screening system that turns public regulatory
and geospatial data into ranked, financially scored micro-hydropower opportunities
at U.S.\ POTW outfalls. Figure~\ref{fig:1} below depicts the top-level dataflow.
Four external source families feed a four-phase Python pipeline; the pipeline
writes parquet intermediates; an export script freezes a 59-property GeoJSON
contract; and a React + MapLibre dashboard reads that single file with no live
backend.

NPV/IRR/payback gates that define the project-viable cohort. The P2-SEED re-baseline

with a physics ceiling of 409.2~GWh/yr, a calibrated band of 89.8--281~GWh/yr,

\subsection{Facility and Permit Tables (EPA ECHO / ICIS-NPDES)}

Three options were considered for the facility universe: (A)~manual municipal
outreach and FOIA per utility, (B)~Clean Watersheds Needs Survey (CWNS) plant
lists without NPDES join keys, and (C)~EPA ECHO bulk ICIS downloads. Option~(A)
cannot reach 17,000 plants in a thesis timeline. Option~(B) lacks the permit and
DMR join path required for measured flow. Option~(C) won because
\texttt{https://echo.epa.gov/files/echodownloads/npdes\_downloads.zip} already
bundles \texttt{ICIS\_FACILITIES.CSV} and \texttt{ICIS\_PERMITS.CSV} with a
stable join key (\texttt{NPDES\_ID} $=$ \texttt{EXTERNAL\_PERMIT\_NMBR}) and is
refreshable without vendor contracts~\cite{epa_echo}.

Implementation lives in \texttt{ingest\_npdes\_facilities} and
\texttt{load\_potw\_facilities}. The ZIP is streamed with retries, extracted once,
and skipped on re-run when the on-disk size matches. Permits are filtered to POTW
facility-type indicators, active status codes (including Administratively Continued
\texttt{ADC}, which recovered 4,308 permits including DC0021199 Blue Plains), and
non-federal treatment plants. Design and actual-average flow fields are scrubbed
before join: values above 2,000~MGD, the EPA numeric sentinel 999.0, and entries
below 0.005~MGD are nulled in \texttt{filter\_potw.py} so they cannot win a
``largest design flow'' deduplication. After the facilities join, P1-COORD-GUARD
drops rows whose latitude/longitude fall outside inclusive U.S.\ NPDES territory
bands (American Samoa, Guam/CNMI, CONUS--Alaska, USVI), rejecting rather than
auto-flipping obvious sign errors so hidden coordinate edits never enter the
parquet. The limitation deferred to future work is outfall-invert elevation: ICIS
gives facility geocodes, not surveyed hydraulic grade lines.

\subsection{Discharge Monitoring Reports (Flow Time Series)}

Three options were considered for flow: (A)~design flow only from the permit table,
(B)~utility SCADA exports, and (C)~national DMR parameter 50050. Design flow alone
cannot support an FDC or capacity-factor story. SCADA does not exist as a public
national archive. DMR won as the only measured monthly flow series covering
essentially the full permitted fleet. Fiscal years 2009--2024 are configured in
\texttt{settings.yaml} under \texttt{epa.dmr\_fiscal\_years}; each year downloads
from \texttt{npdes\_dmrs\_fy\{year\}.zip} with up to four parallel workers in
\texttt{ingest\_dmr\_files}.

Parsing in \texttt{dmr\_timeseries.py} reads each CSV in 500{,}000-row chunks with
\texttt{polars}, keeps parameter code \texttt{50050}, and resolves column-name
aliases that drift across fiscal years. The critical value rule is to prefer
\texttt{DMR\_VALUE\_STANDARD\_UNITS} (EPA-converted MGD) and fall back to
\texttt{DMR\_VALUE\_NMBR} only when the standard-units column is absent; using the
raw number as MGD when facilities report gal/d inflates flow by roughly
$10^{6}$ and was observed to push national means into the thousands of MGD before
the fix. NODI codes map selected ``no data'' indicators to zero or estimated
flags; other NODI values become null. Per-record caps at
$\max(2~\mathrm{MGD},\,10\times\mathrm{design\_flow})$ null corrupt rows before
feature statistics so a single 7{,}000~MGD typo cannot dominate a 0.02~MGD plant.
Primary-outfall selection prefers the outfall with the most non-null months rather
than the maximum mean, which previously let a rarely reported CSO/STR feature beat
the treatment outfall. The named limitation is residual \texttt{dmr\_limited}
sites with fewer than three surviving months: they are excluded or flagged before
Monte Carlo rather than imputed into false precision.

\subsection{Elevation (USGS 3DEP) and Calibration Labels (EHA / EIA)}

Head acquisition compared (A)~literature triangular archetypes only, (B)~paid DEM
tiles processed offline, and (C)~USGS Elevation Point Query Service (EPQS) v1
against plant and outfall coordinates. Archetypes alone are the Phase~2 bootstrap
but were already identified in Chapter~2 as a ranking risk. Offline tiles add
licensing and batch GIS overhead without improving the first national pass.
EPQS won for rebuildability: \texttt{src/phase3/\allowbreak elevation.py} queries
\texttt{https://\allowbreak epqs.\allowbreak nationalmap.\allowbreak gov/\allowbreak v1/\allowbreak json} with \texttt{httpx}/\texttt{asyncio},
honors \texttt{phase3.\allowbreak max\_concurrent\_requests} and \texttt{request\_delay\_s},
caches JSON under \texttt{data/\allowbreak elevation\_cache/} keyed by rounded lat/lon, and
treats the EPQS ocean sentinel $-1{,}000{,}000$ as a failed query~\cite{usgs_3dep}.
Outfall coordinates, when present in
\texttt{NPDES\_PERM\_\allowbreak FEATURE\_\allowbreak COORDS.csv}, enable a plant-minus-outfall DEM proxy;
otherwise the pipeline falls back through literature head then a design default.
The largest methodological assumption in the entire acquisition chain, that DEM
differencing approximates net hydraulic head, is deferred in detail to
Section~4.3.4.

Calibration and ground-truth acquisition compared (A)~no empirical CF check,
(B)~vendor case sheets only, and (C)~DOE HydroSource EHA annual capacity-factor
tables plus EIA-860/923 conventional-hydro generation. Option~(C) won for the
calibration band reported in Chapter~2: EHA supplies 629 plants /
9{,}798 plant-years in the 0.1--5~MW class, ingested through
\texttt{ground\_truth.py} / \texttt{cf\_calibration.py} from
\texttt{ORNL\_\allowbreak EHAHydroPlant\_\allowbreak PublicFY2024.xlsx} and
\texttt{EHA\_\allowbreak Annual\_\allowbreak CapacityFactor.xlsx}~\cite{hydrosource_eha}. EIA Forms 860
and 923 contribute operable hydro nameplate and annual net generation under a
canonical schema, with the documented bias that both sets skew to plants
$\geq$1~MW and are not representative of WWTP micro-scale units. FERC conduit
exemption labels were pursued as the micro-scale supplement and are reported as a
negative result in Chapter~5 rather than silently omitted.

\subsection{Acquisition Honesty and Deferred Work}

The acquisition layer is therefore complete enough to rebuild the screen from
public URLs and on-disk EHA/EIA workbooks, but it is not a clean sensor feed.
Sentinel 999.0 design flows, GPD-as-MGD unit slips, single-row outfall corruption,
and coordinate rows outside U.S.\ territory bands are first-class failure modes
that the code nulls or drops rather than ``fixes'' in place. Even after guards,
head remains a DEM proxy, treatment type is unknown for every plant (energy
intensity uses EPRI observed flow-band averages), and micro-scale generation labels
are too few for supervised learning. Those limits bound what Chapters~4.3--4.5 can
honestly claim. The processing pipeline that consumes these acquired tables will
now be discussed.

\section{Processing Pipeline}                            % WP T3, T4 — Tom

The processing pipeline is responsible for turning the acquired tables of
Section~4.2 into a per-plant screening record: a flow distribution, a ranked
position in the national candidate list, an annual-energy estimate carrying its
own uncertainty, a net head, a matched turbine, and a financial scorecard. It is
implemented as four sequential phases, each writing a parquet file that the next
phase reads, so any phase can be re-run without repeating the ones before it.
Phases~1 and~2 (filtering, flow features, ranking, and Monte-Carlo energy)
are described in Sections~4.3.1 through~4.3.3. Phases~3 and~4 (head
estimation, turbine selection, and cost and financial modeling) follow in
Sections~4.3.4 through~4.3.6. Each subsystem is presented in the same order:
what it is responsible for, the options considered, why the chosen option won,
how it was built, and the limitations deferred to future work.

\subsection{POTW Filtering and Flow Features}

This subsystem is responsible for reducing the national NPDES universe to
publicly owned treatment works that are actually operating, and for converting
their monthly discharge records into one flow distribution per plant. Everything
downstream consumes that distribution, so an error here propagates to every
later number.

Three options were considered for the flow signal itself. Option~(A) was the
permitted design flow alone, a single scalar available for most plants.
Option~(B) was the arithmetic mean of the reported monthly average flows.
Option~(C) was a 20-point flow duration curve (FDC) evaluated at fixed
exceedance probabilities from 0.01 to 0.95. Option~(A) was rejected because
design flow is a permit ceiling rather than an observation, and utilization at
U.S.\ plants varies widely enough that a design-flow screen would rank permit
paperwork rather than water. Option~(B) was rejected because hydropower energy
is an integral over the whole flow distribution: two plants with the same mean
flow but different variability produce different annual energy and require
different turbines. Option~(C) won because it preserves the shape of the
distribution at a fixed, comparable grid, feeds the trapezoidal energy
integration of Section~4.3.3 directly, and supplies the reliable low-flow
baseline ($Q_{P10}$) used in ranking.

A second choice was required for plants reporting several outfalls. The options
were (A)~summing all outfalls, (B)~keeping the outfall with the highest mean
flow, and (C)~keeping the outfall with the most non-null monthly records, with
combined-sewer, storm, and emergency prefixes pushed last and the mean used only
as a tie-breaker. Summing double-counts plants that report the same water at a
process point and a final discharge point. The maximum-mean rule was the
original implementation and failed in production: a single corrupted row on a
rarely reported overflow outfall could beat dozens of clean records on the real
treatment outfall. Option~(C) replaced it and is what \texttt{flow\_features.py}
implements today.

Implementation runs in two modules. \texttt{filter\_potw.py} keeps permits whose
facility-type indicator is \texttt{POTW} or \texttt{POT}, whose status code is
effective, non-major effective, or administratively continued, and which are not
federal treatment facilities; nulls the EPA sentinel value 999.0, any flow above
2{,}000~MGD, and any actual-average flow exceeding five times design flow;
deduplicates to one row per NPDES identifier keeping the largest surviving design
flow; joins the facility table for name, state, and coordinates; and applies the
P1-COORD-GUARD territory bands described in Section~4.2.1.
\texttt{flow\_features.py} then groups the DMR series by plant, filters
individual monthly readings above five times design flow \emph{before} any
statistic is computed, and returns mean, median, standard deviation, coefficient
of variation, the 10th, 25th, 75th, and 90th percentiles, minimum, maximum,
month and year counts, missing-record fraction, a seasonal amplitude taken as
the spread of calendar-month means, a linear flow trend from
\texttt{scipy.stats.linregress}, and the FDC itself. The FDC is built from
Weibull plotting positions $i/(n+1)$ on the descending sorted record and
interpolated onto the 20-point exceedance grid held in
\texttt{settings.yaml}. Each plant is finally tagged with a data-quality tier:
\texttt{dmr} for twelve or more surviving months, \texttt{dmr\_limited} below
that, \texttt{actual\_avg\_only} when the permit's actual-average field is the
sole flow signal, and \texttt{design\_only} when mean flow has to be inferred as
75~\% of design flow. Across the 17{,}148 plants that survive Phase~1, the tiers
split 11{,}555 \texttt{dmr}, 607 \texttt{dmr\_limited}, 2{,}779
\texttt{actual\_avg\_only}, and 2{,}207 \texttt{design\_only}.

The per-reading filter is itself a result worth reporting. The original guard
compared the finished mean against five times design flow and nulled every
statistic when the ratio failed. That rule silently destroyed 435 plants whose
records were mostly clean: their median record count was 110 months, and in 407
cases a single bogus month (for example 300~MGD filed against a 0.252~MGD
plant) had inflated the mean past the cap. Moving the same threshold from the
aggregate to the individual reading recovered those 407 plants with real FDCs,
while the 20 plants whose readings were bogus throughout still fall through to
the design-flow fallback. The aggregate check was kept as a safety net for rows
whose design flow is null at filter time.

Three limitations are deferred. First, DMR reports monthly averages, so the FDC
describes month-to-month variation and cannot represent diurnal swing; peak
instantaneous flow at a plant is higher than any point on the curve, and the
low-flow tail is correspondingly smoother than reality. Second, the
primary-outfall rule assumes one outfall carries the recoverable discharge, which
is wrong for plants that split flow across parallel outfalls. Third, the 75~\%
utilization factor applied to \texttt{design\_only} plants is an assumption, not
a measurement; those 2{,}207 plants carry it into every downstream number and are
flagged by data-quality tier so they can be excluded from any later analysis that
requires measured flow.

How those flow features are combined into a single review order will now be discussed.

\subsection{Composite Ranking}

Ranking is responsible for ordering the 17{,}148 screened plants before any
expensive per-site work is done, so that manual review, elevation queries, and
later validation effort start where the evidence is strongest.

Three options were considered. Option~(A) was ordering by mean flow alone.
Option~(B) was ordering by a first-pass energy estimate. Option~(C) was a
weighted composite of several normalized flow features. Option~(A) is a single
axis and treats a plant with six months of erratic records identically to one
with sixteen years of stable records at the same mean. Option~(B) is
circular at this stage: energy requires head, and head does not exist until
Phase~3; ranking on a head assumption would rank the assumption. Option~(C)
won because every input it needs is already measured in Phase~1, and because the
weights are explicit and auditable rather than buried inside an energy model.
The weights are given in Table~\ref{tab:rank_weights}.

Implementation is in \texttt{ranking.py}. Each component is min-max normalized
across the fleet with a $10^{-12}$ term added to the denominator so a degenerate
column cannot divide by zero, then combined by the weights above, which are
re-normalized to sum to 1.0 at load so a partial override in

signal ranks last on that axis instead of scoring as average. Plants whose
design flow is explicitly zero (misclassified industrial dischargers that
carry high DMR flow, of which KYP000044 is the documented example) have their
score forced to zero, while plants whose design flow is merely null keep a normal
score, because nulling those would zero 2{,}106 sites that are probably
genuine treatment works with incomplete permit records. The final ordinal rank is
assigned by descending score.

Two limitations are named. The weights are engineering judgment, not fitted
parameters: no labeled outcome existed to fit them against, which is the same
scarcity that ends the machine-learning attempt reported in Section~4.4. And
because normalization is fleet-relative, a ranking score is only comparable
within one pipeline run. Neither limitation propagates into the energy or
financial results, because ranking is not a gate: all 17{,}148 plants pass to
Phase~2 regardless of rank, so a mis-weighted score costs review order, not
exclusion.

The conversion of those flow features into an annual-energy estimate with an explicit
uncertainty band is described next.

\subsection{Monte-Carlo Energy Estimation}

Phase~2 is responsible for converting each plant's flow duration curve into an
annual-energy estimate that carries its own uncertainty, and for excluding plants
that cannot support a turbine before any per-site network or optimization work is
spent on them. It applies the hydropower relation of Equation~\eqref{eq:hydro} with
the constants held in \texttt{settings.yaml}: water density 998.2~kg/m\textsuperscript{3},
gravitational acceleration 9.81~m/s\textsuperscript{2}, the conversion
1~MGD~$=$~0.043813~m\textsuperscript{3}/s, and 8{,}766~h/yr.

Three options were considered for handling uncertainty. Option~(A) was a
deterministic point estimate at mean flow, default efficiency, and a default
head. Option~(B) was a low, base, and high scenario triplet. Option~(C) was
Monte-Carlo sampling with 10{,}000 iterations per plant. Option~(A) was rejected
because the dominant unknown at this stage is head, which has not been measured
yet, and a point estimate presents an assumption with the same authority as a
measurement. Option~(B) improves on that but yields three numbers whose
probability content is undefined; there is no basis for calling the high
scenario a P90. Option~(C) won because it produces genuine percentiles from
stated input distributions, costs about 10~ms per plant once vectorized, and
makes the uncertainty of the screen reportable rather than implied.

Each iteration draws three independent samples. Head is drawn from a triangular
distribution selected by design-flow archetype, as listed in
Table~\ref{tab:head_archetypes}. Turbine efficiency is drawn from
Triangular(0.70, 0.82, 0.90), pooled across machine types because no turbine has
been selected at this stage. Plant availability is drawn from Triangular(0.90,
0.95, 0.98). Power is evaluated at each of the 20 FDC points, integrated over the
exceedance axis by the trapezoidal rule, and multiplied by 8{,}766~h/yr and the
availability sample to give net annual energy. The integration is performed as a
single $10{,}000 \times 20$ matrix operation in \texttt{energy\_physics.py}
rather than a Python loop, and \texttt{monte\_carlo.py} distributes plants across
worker processes with \texttt{concurrent.\allowbreak futures.\allowbreak ProcessPoolExecutor} when more
than one worker is requested.

Seeding was rebuilt after a reproducibility failure that is worth recording.
The original implementation seeded each plant with the base seed plus its row
index. When P1-COORD-GUARD removed ten bad-coordinate facilities and the fleet
went from 17{,}158 to 17{,}148 rows, every plant after the first removed row
received a different seed, 1{,}090 financial scorecard rows were re-drawn, and
three viability calls flipped, none of which had anything to do with the ten
removed plants. The replacement, \texttt{\_site\_seed\_sequence}, derives each
plant's seed from the first eight bytes of the SHA-256 digest of its NPDES
identifier mixed with the base seed through
\texttt{numpy.random.SeedSequence}. The Python built-in \texttt{hash} was not
used because it is salted per process. Draws are now invariant to row order, row
count, and worker count, and the sequential and parallel paths produce identical
output; changing the base seed remains the deliberate way to re-baseline the
whole fleet.

A three-test exclusion gate runs before sampling, in priority order. Plants with
null, zero, or negative mean flow are excluded as \texttt{no\_usable\_flow};
plants below the 0.5~MGD viability threshold are excluded as \texttt{small\_potw};
and \texttt{dmr\_limited} plants with fewer than three surviving months are
excluded as \texttt{sparse\_dmr\_artifact}. Of the 17{,}148 plants entering
Phase~2, 1{,}496 fail the first test, 10{,}182 the second, and 6 the third,
leaving 5{,}464 retained. Excluded plants are written to the output parquet with
their reason string rather than dropped, so the funnel of Section~5.1.2 can be
recomputed from the file itself.

For each retained plant the phase writes the 10th, 50th, and 90th energy
percentiles, the sample mean and standard deviation, the median power, the
implied capacity factor, the three head percentiles, and an equivalent-households
figure at 10{,}500~kWh/yr per U.S.\ home~\cite{eia_household}. Summed over the
5{,}464 retained plants the P50 estimate is 699.18~GWh/yr, against 444.31~GWh/yr
at P10 and 1{,}002.13~GWh/yr at P90. The median plant returns 24{,}146~kWh/yr at
P50, and the median ratio of P90 to P10 within a single plant is 2.28, which is
the width the head assumption alone imposes on a site before any measurement is
taken. Figure~\ref{fig:mc_energy} shows both halves of that result: the
10{,}000-sample distribution for one representative plant and the fleet
distribution of per-plant P50 energy.

Four limitations are named. The head triangulars are archetypes indexed by
design flow, not site measurements, and no plant-specific evidence supports the
mode values; Section~4.3.4 replaces them with a digital-elevation-model estimate
and flags that estimate as the largest methodological assumption in the work.
The three sampled quantities are drawn independently, so any real correlation
between head, machine efficiency, and availability is absent from the reported
spread. The FDC integration is truncated to the shorter of the curve and the
exceedance grid, which drops the low-flow tail for plants with sparse records and
understates their annual energy by an estimated few per cent. Finally, the
implied capacity factor is implausibly high: across the 5{,}464 retained plants
its median is 0.869 (tenth to ninetieth percentile 0.834 to 0.883), and across
the 1{,}138 plants that survive to project viability in Phase~4 it is 0.872
(0.856 to 0.883). Part of that is real (treated wastewater discharge is far
flatter than river flow), and part is optimism, because a smooth monthly FDC is
integrated as though the machine ran at every point on it with no debris, minimum
flow, or outage modeling. That discrepancy is
not corrected inside Phase~2; it is measured against real capacity factors and
turned into the calibration band in Section~4.4. Head estimation, which removes
the archetype assumption these energy figures rest on, will now be discussed.
\subsection{Head Estimation}

Phase~3 is responsible for replacing the archetype head of Section~4.3.3 with a
site-specific value, and for deciding which plants have enough vertical drop to
be worth sizing a machine for. Head enters Equation~\eqref{eq:hydro} linearly and
is the one term in it that no acquired dataset reports, so this subsystem carries
the largest methodological assumption in the work.

Three options were considered. Option~(A) was to keep the Phase~2 triangular
archetypes. Option~(B) was the difference between the ground elevation at the
plant and at its permitted discharge point, sampled from a national digital
elevation model. Option~(C) was to compute head hydraulically from as-built
drawings: effluent channel water surface, outfall pipe invert, receiving water
level. Option~(A) is the thing being replaced: a national median with no site
geography in it, and Section~4.3.3 showed it alone imposes a median P90-to-P10
ratio of 2.28 on a plant's energy. Option~(C) is the correct engineering answer
and was rejected on availability, because no national dataset of outfall invert
elevations exists and drawings for 17{,}148 plants are not a screening activity.
Option~(B) won because it is the only site-specific option available nationally
at no marginal cost, and because its failures are detectable: a wrong coordinate
usually produces a negative or absurd drop rather than a plausible wrong number.

Implementation runs in three modules. \texttt{outfall\_coords.py} resolves one
discharge coordinate per permit, preferring the rows of the NPDES outfalls layer
whose feature type is an external outfall (actual discharge-pipe locations)
and consulting the generic permit-feature coordinate file only for permits absent
from that layer, because it does not label feature type and its rows are often
the facility registration point rather than the pipe. Within either source the
feature numbered \texttt{001} is preferred, then the lowest numeric feature, then
the first row, and null or geographically implausible coordinates are dropped.
\texttt{elevation.py} queries the USGS 3DEP Elevation Point Query
Service~\cite{usgs_3dep} in WGS-84 for elevations in meters, batching through
\texttt{asyncio} and \texttt{httpx} at ten concurrent connections with a 0.5~s
delay, and caching every response on disk under its coordinate rounded to five
decimals so re-runs are free and reproducible. Of the 5{,}464 plants entering
Phase~3, 5{,}461 facility elevations came from that cache and 3 queries failed;
outfall elevations resolved for 5{,}178 plants. \texttt{head\_estimation.py} takes
gross head as facility elevation minus outfall elevation and net head as
$H_{net} = H_{gross}(1 - 0.15)$, the loss fraction standing for pipe friction,
valves, bends, and screen losses.

The plausibility gate is the substance of this subsystem and runs in a fixed
order. A candidate at or below zero means the outfall sits at or above the plant,
which is impossible for a gravity outfall, so the reading is discarded and the
archetype used. A candidate between zero and the 1.0~m minimum is kept as
measured and the plant marked head-invalid: the data is saying the site lacks
head, and overwriting it with a 5~m design assumption would manufacture viable
projects. A candidate above 1.0~m is accepted unless it diverges from the
archetype by more than four times the archetype value. That limit was originally
two, and was raised once it was found to reject legitimate 10--20~m readings at
Appalachian and mountain-west plants whose real relief exceeds a national median
by design. Elevation heads are labeled high confidence and archetype heads
medium confidence, and both labels travel to the export layer of Section~4.5.

above the one-to-one line at 36.8~\% of plants and at a median ratio of
$0.77\times$. Neither series is a measurement, and the figure is included so that the
reader can see the size of the disagreement rather than take the assumption on trust.

Five limitations are named, and the first is why this subsystem is flagged as the
work's largest assumption. The 1/3~arc-second national elevation data behind the
query service has a published overall absolute vertical accuracy of 2.44~m RMSE
against roughly 25{,}000 geodetic control points~\cite{usgs_ned_accuracy}, while
head here is a \emph{difference} of two such values and the median accepted head
is 4.144~m. Errors at two points a few hundred meters apart are spatially
correlated, so the error on their difference is smaller than the
$\sqrt{2} \times 2.44 = 3.45$~m bound independent errors would give, but no
site-specific error bound was computed, and the honest reading is that per-site
head is an order-of-magnitude screen rather than a measurement. The negative
readings are evidence of the same thing: their median inversion is $-0.53$~m of
gross head, inside the noise, while the worst is $-2{,}258$~m, which is a
coordinate on a hilltop. Second, the service returns terrain elevation, not the
water surface in the effluent channel nor the outfall pipe invert, so submerged
outfalls, tailwater, and backwater are invisible to it. Third, the 0.15 loss
fraction is one constant applied to every plant rather than a value computed from
pipe length, diameter, and roughness. Fourth, 1{,}682 plants, and 360 of the
1{,}138 project-viable sites of Chapter~5, still carry the archetype head, so
their economics rest on the assumption this phase exists to remove. Fifth, the
deferred fix for the negative-head cohort is a snap of the outfall coordinate
onto the nearest National Hydrography Dataset flowline centerline, whose endpoint
is always on the receiving stream and therefore always below the plant; it was
scoped and not built. What this assumption is worth in dollars is quantified in
Section~4.3.6. Turbine selection, which consumes this head, will now be
discussed.

\subsection{Turbine Selection}

This subsystem is responsible for choosing a machine class for each plant's
head-flow operating point, sizing its rated flow, and recomputing annual energy
with that machine's part-load behavior rather than the pooled efficiency
distribution of Phase~2.

Three options were considered. Option~(A) was a single generic machine at fixed
efficiency. Option~(B) was rule-based type selection from the standard head-flow
envelope, a type-specific part-load curve, and a sweep over rated flow.
Option~(C) was per-site optimization against a catalogue of real machine models.
Option~(A) was rejected because part-load behavior is what separates machine
classes at these heads (an in-conduit unit holds a flat 75~\% where a Kaplan
holds 93~\% at rating), and the difference compounds over every point of a flow
duration curve. Option~(C) was rejected because manufacturers publish an
operating envelope and a price band, not a performance curve per model, so
optimizing against the catalogue would fit vendor marketing rather than physics.
Option~(B) won because its rules are auditable and it needs nothing the pipeline
does not already hold.

Implementation is in \texttt{turbine\_selection.py}. Type follows a decision tree
evaluated in order: Pelton when $H > 50$~m and
$Q < 2$~m\textsuperscript{3}/s; Francis when $H \geq 10$~m; Crossflow when
$2 \leq H < 10$~m and $Q < 0.5$~m\textsuperscript{3}/s; Kaplan when
$2 \leq H < 10$~m and $Q \geq 0.5$~m\textsuperscript{3}/s; and an in-conduit
micro-turbine below 2~m. Efficiency is a function of the part-load ratio
$q = Q/Q_{rated}$: Kaplan follows $-0.30q^2 + 0.60q + 0.63$ capped at 0.93,
Francis $-0.50q^2 + 0.90q + 0.45$ capped at 0.91 and reaching 0.85 at rating,
Crossflow $-0.10q^2 + 0.20q + 0.72$ capped at 0.82 and zero below $q = 0.10$,
Pelton a flat 0.88 above the 0.15 nozzle minimum, and the in-conduit unit a flat
0.75 above $q = 0.10$. The optimizer sweeps rated flow from 0.3 to 1.0 times
design flow, integrates $\eta(q)\,\rho g Q H$ over the Phase~1 flow duration
curve by the trapezoidal rule at each candidate, discards ratings below a 0.40
capacity factor or 1~kW of rated power, and keeps the rating yielding the most
annual energy. A manufacturer is matched on type with the operating point inside
the published flow and head envelope, preferring vendors listing wastewater
certification and then the lowest published cost per kilowatt; when no envelope
contains the point the match relaxes to type alone, so the field records that a
commercial machine class exists rather than that a model was quoted.

Five limitations are named, and the first is a negative result about our own
design. The rated-flow sweep is very nearly a no-op: 3{,}771 of 3{,}778 plants
are rated at 100~\% of design flow, because undersizing raises capacity factor
but caps the top of the flow duration curve, and against an energy-maximizing
objective the full rating almost always wins. The sweep therefore changes the
answer at seven sites while costing run time at all of them, and is worth keeping
only once the objective becomes economic rather than energetic (maximum net
present value would trade clipped energy against a smaller machine), which is
deferred. Second, the 1{,}082 head-valid plants that fail here split into 977
below the 1~kW rated-power floor and 105 below the 0.40 capacity-factor floor;
seven of the 977 store a rated power that rounds to 1.00~kW in the output file
but were sized just under the floor, a reporting artifact of writing that column
at two decimals. Third, the efficiency curves are quadratic fits to published
hill charts, not vendor-certified performance on screened wastewater, and carry
no term for debris, screen fouling, or grit wear. Fourth, the manufacturer field
is a nearest-envelope lookup against 15 machine lines from 10 manufacturers and
must not be read as a quotation or a compatibility assessment. Fifth, the Pelton
curve and its cost coefficients are untested, because the corpus never selects
one. The cost and financial layer that consumes these ratings will now be
discussed.

\subsection{Cost Models and Financial Scorecard}

Phase~4 is responsible for turning a sized machine into a financial verdict: what
the project costs to build and run, what its output is worth, and whether it
clears an investment gate. It also holds the least defensible numbers in the
pipeline, so its provenance is reported alongside its results.

Three options were considered for equipment cost. Option~(A) was a flat cost per
kilowatt. Option~(B) was a power law
$\mathrm{CapEx}/\mathrm{kW} = A \cdot \mathrm{kW}^{B}$ clamped per machine type.
Option~(C) was a vendor quotation per site. Option~(A) was rejected because the
cost story at this scale is the scale effect itself (a 3~kW machine and a
500~kW machine differ by roughly a factor of three per kilowatt), so a flat rate
would either bankrupt every micro site or gift every large one. Option~(C) is
what a developer would do for one project and is unavailable for 3{,}778, since
manufacturers publish bands rather than quotations. Option~(B) won, in the form
used by the national hydropower cost literature~\cite{doe_vision, ornl2014}, with
the vendor bands kept as an independent cross-check on the model rather than as
the model.

Implementation is in \texttt{cost\_models.py} and \texttt{financials.py} with all
coefficients in \texttt{settings.yaml}. Equipment cost per kilowatt is the power
law clamped to a per-type floor and ceiling, as listed in
Table~\ref{tab:cost_params}. Installation is 17.5~\% of equipment cost, the
midpoint of the 15--20~\% range recommended by the project's industry adviser,
and its scope is stated narrowly as mechanical labour: it excludes civil works
and does not overlap interconnection or permitting, which are separate lines.
Because installation is one-time, operations and maintenance stays a percentage of
\emph{equipment} cost alone, from 1.5~\% for Pelton to 3.0~\% for in-conduit
machines. Interconnection is \$5{,}000 at or below 25~kW, on the reasoning that a
micro turbine at an outfall offsets its own plant's load behind its own meter and
so needs a non-export relay, a disconnect, and utility notification rather than a
distribution tie, an export power purchase agreement, and revenue-grade export
metering; above 25~kW it steps through \$50{,}000, \$100{,}000, \$150{,}000, and
\$200{,}000. Permitting is \$5{,}000 at or below 25~kW because a turbine on a
municipal wastewater conduit is a qualifying conduit hydropower facility needing
only a Notice of Intent with no application fee and no
exhibits~\cite{ferc_conduit}, then \$75{,}000 to 250~kW for abbreviated review and
\$150{,}000 above it for full licensing with environmental review.

The financial model is a 30-year discounted cash flow at a 6~\% real discount
rate with 0.2~\%/yr output degradation. Revenue is annual energy times the sum of
the plant's state industrial electricity rate, taken from 2023 state
averages~\cite{eia_rates}, and a \$0.01/kWh renewable energy certificate value;
the median viable site sees \$0.0740/kWh. Net present value is the discounted
cash-flow sum less capital cost, internal rate of return is solved by Brent's
method from $-99$~\% to $+300$~\% with explicit sentinels written when no sign
change exists in that interval, payback is simple and undiscounted, and levelized
cost is capital cost plus the present value of operations over the present value
of energy. A site is project-viable when net present value is positive, payback is
20~years or less, and the rate of return is a real solver result rather than a
sentinel; that exclusion matters, because a nano-scale project whose return
pegs above 300~\% is an artifact of a tiny denominator, not an investment. A
minimum annual revenue floor of \$20{,}000 previously gated viability; it was
removed by project decision so sites are judged on cash flow alone, and the lever
is kept at zero in configuration so the gate stays documented and reversible.

Two corrections to this model are results in their own right. The vendor
cross-check compares the power-law cost per kilowatt against the envelope
published by the manufacturers of each machine type. On first run it flagged
1{,}019 of 3{,}783 sites, or 26.9~\%: 989 Crossflow sites above the \$6{,}000/kW
vendor ceiling at a model median of \$6{,}670/kW, and 30 Francis sites below the
\$1{,}800/kW floor. Two clamp changes (the Crossflow ceiling from \$7{,}500 to
\$6{,}000 and the Francis floor from \$700 to \$1{,}800) took the count to
zero, as Figure~\ref{fig:capex} shows, and raised portfolio capital cost by
\$7.2M because those Francis sites had been genuinely under-costed against real
vendor pricing. The exponents resisted the same treatment. Only the in-conduit
coefficients were refit, against the ORNL conduit capital-cost
equation~\cite{ornl2014} sampled at 44 power-head points, giving $A = 20{,}283$
and $B = -0.181$ at $R^2 = 0.89$. Fits from the published European
equipment-cost equations for small hydro~\cite{ogayar2009} were rejected for the
other four types because they priced 150 of 463 Francis sites and 3 of 385 Kaplan
sites below United States vendor floors, and because United States sources at this
scale report total installed cost including civil works rather than equipment-only
cost~\cite{osti3002705}. Those four exponents therefore remain literature-form and
unvalidated against United States installations; closing that gap needs at least
three real equipment-only project costs per machine type and is deferred.

2{,}803 of the 3{,}778 scored sites. This is the price of the Section~4.3.4

One further column sets expectations. Each site's generation is compared against
an estimate of its own plant's consumption, derived from published treatment
energy-intensity curves~\cite{epri2013, wef_mop32} at the plant's mean flow. The
median viable site offsets 2.016~\% of its plant's consumption and the median
across all scored sites is 1.104~\%. Recovery at an outfall is a trim on a
treatment plant's electricity bill, not a path to self-sufficiency, and any
business case built on these results has to be built at that scale.

Six limitations are named. The power-law exponents for four of five machine types
are literature-form and unvalidated against United States installations, and the
operations-and-maintenance percentages are industry rules of thumb with no
derivation at all. The model carries no financing structure, tax credit, or grant
beyond a single scenario column recomputing net present value at 50~\% capital
subsidy. Civil works are excluded by scope, so a site needing a wet well, a
bypass, or structural work on the outfall is under-costed here. Electricity rates
are single-year state averages held flat in real terms for 30~years, which
suppresses both escalation and the tariff structure a municipal customer would
face. Payback and levelized cost are written with a sentinel value where they are
infinite, and a downstream consumer treating that sentinel as a number will
misread it. Finally, 290 of the 1{,}138 viable sites have no measured flow
duration curve, so their head and flow sensitivities collapse to the same linear
energy scaling and are reported as inseparable rather than as two independent
bands. How the energy figures behind all of these economics were tested against
real generating plants is the subject of Section~4.4.

\section{Calibration and Validation}                     % WP T5 — Tom

Everything reported to this point is modeled. Phases~1--4 turn public records
into an energy figure and a cash flow for 3{,}778 plants without comparing one
number against an operating machine, and no WOWERS site has been instrumented.
This section reports the two attempts made at that comparison: a benchmark of the
model's implied capacity factor against measured generation, in Section~4.4.1,
and an abandoned attempt to learn a correction from the same data, in
Section~4.4.2. Neither is validation. The first produces a bound, and it is that
bound, rather than the physics point estimate, that this work claims.

\subsection{Capacity-Factor Calibration}

The Monte-Carlo estimate of Section~4.3.3 embeds one scalar that carries every
operating assumption it makes: the capacity factor, here the ratio of median
annual energy to the energy the same site would make at its median-condition
power (median head, median efficiency, mean flow) held for every one of the
8{,}766~h the pipeline counts in a year. Across the 1{,}138 project-viable sites
the Phase~2 estimate implies a
median capacity factor of 0.8725, with a tenth-to-ninetieth-percentile spread of
0.8556--0.8833. That describes a turbine at or near rated output for 87~\% of the
year, every year. This subsystem is responsible for asking whether that number
survives contact with plants that exist, and for restating the headline as a band
where it does not.

Four options were considered. Option~(A) was direct measurement at a WWTP outfall,
which is the correct answer and is out of scope for a desk study with no hardware
budget. Option~(B) was comparison against published national assessments such as
the DOE hydropower resource work~\cite{doe_vision, ornl2014}; those are modeled
estimates too, so the comparison would set one model against another and measure
nothing. Option~(C) was to re-tune the Phase~2 sampling distributions until the
implied capacity factor matched real plants. Option~(D) was to hold the physics
model fixed and benchmark its implied capacity factor against measured annual
capacity factors from operating plants, publishing the ratio as a multiplier
band. Option~(C) was rejected because it buries the correction inside the model,
where a reader cannot see it and cannot undo it. Option~(D) won on three grounds:
measured capacity factors exist at scale and at no cost, the capacity factor is
exactly where the optimism of Section~4.3.3 lives, and a published multiplier is
reversible: dividing by it returns the physics number.

Where 0.8725 comes from is worth stating before it is benchmarked, because part
of it is defensible. It decomposes as availability times flow-duration-curve
utilization, $0.943 \times 0.925 = 0.872$. The first term is the mean of the
Triangular(0.90, 0.95, 0.98) availability sampled in Section~4.3.3. The second is
a property of the input data rather than an assumption: municipal discharge
tracks population, not rainfall, so the duration curve is nearly flat and a
turbine rated at design flow sits near its rating almost continuously. The flat
curve is real. The 0.95 modal availability, and the absence of any minimum-flow
cutoff or debris-fouling model, are not.

The calibration is implemented in \texttt{scripts/cf\_calibration.py}, which is
strictly read-only: it prints tables and writes no parquet, config, or source
file. Its reference data is the DOE HydroSource EHA annual capacity-factor
workbook covering 2005--2022~\cite{hydrosource_eha}, whose net-generation column
is the figure each plant reports to the Energy Information Administration on Form
EIA-923~\cite{eia923}. Capacity factor is recomputed from net generation, plant
capacity, and hours rather than read from the workbook's own percentage string;
the two agree to a mean absolute difference of 0.00250 across 23{,}483 rows,
which is rounding and confirms the arithmetic. Rows are then filtered to
conventional hydro, capacity of at least 0.1~MW, and a recomputed capacity factor
above zero and no greater than 1.2, the last two dropping reporting artifacts.
Three buckets are cut: a primary 0.1--5~MW bucket of 629 plants and 9{,}798
plant-years with a median capacity factor of 0.3902, a 0.1--1~MW bucket closest
in scale to a WWTP machine at 59 plants and 802 plant-years and 0.4145, and a
2013--2022 slice of the primary bucket, run as a stability check, at 611 plants
and 5{,}530 plant-years and 0.3817. The empirical figure is therefore not an
artifact of a particular decade.

The two distributions are drawn together in Figure~\ref{fig:cfcalib}. They barely
overlap, and the gap has two causes that must not be conflated. Part of it is
genuine: river plants are sized for flood flows and idle through droughts, their
inflow varies by an order of magnitude across a year against roughly 15--25~\%
for municipal discharge, and many carry license conditions releasing ecological
minimum flows around the turbine, none of which applies at an outfall. The rest
is the optimism named above. Because the first part is real, the river-hydro
figure is treated throughout as a floor and never as a best estimate.

The upper tier needs a plant that shares the hydraulics, and one qualifies. The
Conduit~3 Hydroelectric Project in Portland, Oregon runs four spherical turbines
inside a 1.07~m drinking-water transmission main. Its Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission application under docket P-14498 gives a nameplate capacity of 170~kW
and an estimated 1{,}200~MWh/yr~\cite{ferc_p14498}; the as-built system is
reported at 200~kW and an expected 1{,}100~MWh/yr~\cite{lucidpipe}. Those pairs
imply capacity factors of $1{,}200{,}000 \div (170 \times 8{,}760) = 0.806$ and
$1{,}100{,}000 \div (200 \times 8{,}760) = 0.628$ respectively. Both are design
estimates, not metered generation: at 170--200~kW the plant falls below the 1~MW
threshold for Form EIA-923 reporting~\cite{eia923}, so it has no public annual
generation record, which is the same reporting gap that ends the Phase~5 effort in
Section~4.4.2. It is drinking water rather
than wastewater, but the hydraulic case is the same: continuous pressurized
conduit flow, no seasonal drought, no minimum-flow reservation. The anchor
was set at 0.60, below the whole 0.628--0.806 documented range, to
acknowledge that one project is a thin basis, that its generation figures are
projections rather than measurements, and that the Phase~2 optimism is real. Rentricity and
CINK both operate in-conduit machines on potable and wastewater
systems~\cite{rentricity, cink} and both confirm the operating regime, but
neither publishes annual energy per site, so they widen confidence in the range
without setting a number in it.

That anchor is a projection, and the honest question is what measurement says. It
says something less comfortable. The EHA Canal/Conduit class contains 115 plants whose
annual generation is metered and filed on Form EIA-923~\cite{hydrosource_eha, eia923},
and their median capacity factor is 0.2439, which maps to 114.4~GWh/yr on this
portfolio. Four of those plants carry continuous municipal or wastewater duty rather
than seasonal irrigation flow, and they are the closest operating analogs to a WWTP
outfall that exist in the United States: R~E~Badger Filtration Plant at 1{,}400~kW and
297~MWh in 2013 (CF 0.0242), Ruxton Park at 1{,}000~kW and 1{,}388~MWh in 2022
(0.1584), Point Loma at 1{,}500~kW and 2{,}515~MWh in 2017 (0.1914), and Manitou
Springs at 5{,}600~kW and 18{,}102~MWh in 2022 (0.3690). Point Loma is the only
treated-wastewater conduit plant in the set and therefore the single most on-point
figure in this entire calibration; it sits at 0.1914, not near 0.60. Three of the four
fall below 0.20, and the Badger figure is low enough that a partial or curtailed
reporting year is the likely explanation rather than a genuine annual capacity factor.

Three things follow, and they do not all point the same way. First, the floor tier is
now supported twice over from independent data: metered conduit plants give
114.4~GWh/yr against 118.9~GWh/yr from the EHA river-hydro first quartile, a
4.5~GWh/yr agreement between two datasets that share no plants and no methodology.
That is the strongest evidential result in this section. Second, the 0.60 tier has no
measured support at any scale, and the obvious rescue does not work: if capacity factor
rose as capacity fell, extrapolating upward to a 12.96~kW median site would have an
empirical basis, but across the metered population the correlation between capacity
factor and $\log_{10}$ of nameplate is $+0.093$, and the smallest quartile,
500--1{,}800~kW, has the \emph{lowest} median capacity factor at 0.2101. There is no
downward-scale trend to extrapolate along. Third, the comparison is nonetheless not
like for like, and saying so is not special pleading: every metered plant is at least
500~kW against a WOWERS median viable site of 12.96~kW, so the 0.60 tier is an
extrapolation two orders of magnitude outside the measured range in either direction,
and 111 of the 115 plants are seasonal irrigation canal drops whose duty cycle is
exactly the variability a municipal outfall does not have.

The 0.60 tier is therefore retained but relabeled. It is reported in this work as an
\emph{optimistic scenario} resting on one project's design projection and on an
argument from design rather than from evidence: WOWERS sizes rated flow at a point on
each plant's measured flow duration curve, where a retrofit into an existing main
inherits whatever nameplate the original installer chose, so a purpose-sized machine
should hold a higher capacity factor than an oversized one. That argument is plausible
and it is untested. It is not called a central estimate anywhere in this thesis, and
the figure this work stands behind is the band rather than its upper end.

The reported band floor is lowered accordingly, from 118.9 to 89.8~GWh/yr, and the
reasoning deserves stating because it cuts against the argument of the preceding
paragraph. The objection is obvious: 89.8~GWh/yr rests on one plant, and a single
observation is exactly the thin basis just criticized in the upward direction. The
answer is that the two thin bases are not thin in the same way. The 0.60 tier
extrapolates from a design projection in the direction that flatters the result; the
89.8 figure is a metered annual generation figure from the only plant in the country
that is actually a treated-wastewater conduit installation, and it points in the
direction that does not flatter it. Where the correct plant class contains exactly one
measurement, reporting that measurement as the floor is more defensible than reporting
a quartile of a plant class this work has already argued is the wrong one. The floor is
therefore the measured Point Loma figure, and the reader is given the whole ladder in
Table~\ref{tab:cfband} rather than a single number: 89.8~GWh/yr from one plant in the
right class, 114.4~GWh/yr from 115 plants in a related class, and 118.9~GWh/yr from 629
plants in a class that is not ours. All three sit within 30~GWh/yr of one another, which
is the substantive point: the floor is insensitive to which of the three defensible
choices is made, and the band's width is driven almost entirely by its upper end.

Five limitations are named. First, the floor imports variability that outfalls do
not have, so a portfolio held to it is being asked to perform no better than a
run-of-river plant in a dry year; it is a defensible worst case and nothing
stronger. Second, the optimistic tier rests on one project, in drinking water, at a
generation figure this work did not measure, and the four metered municipal-conduit
plants that could have supported it instead sit between 0.0242 and 0.3690, so the
tier survives as a scenario and not as an estimate. Third, the reference workbook covers
plants of about 1~MW and above, so the bucket nearest WWTP scale is 59 plants,
while the median viable WOWERS machine is rated at 3.80~kW, three orders of
magnitude below the calibration set, and the gap is not closed by any data
currently published. Fourth, the multiplier assumes energy scales with capacity
factor at a fixed rating, which holds inside this model and has not been tested
outside it. Fifth, and most important, this is calibration of a single scalar
against somebody else's plants, not validation of any site in the portfolio; the
quantity being checked is a fleet-level plausibility, and no claim is made here
about any individual facility. The calibrated result is reported in
Section~5.1.4. Why a learned correction was not built instead will now be
discussed.

\subsection{Machine-Learning Feasibility}

The original architecture reserved a fifth phase for a supervised model that would
learn the residual between the physics estimate and measured generation, and would
supply the physics-versus-measured check the rest of the pipeline lacks. That
phase was designed, its data path was built, and it was then killed. This
subsection records that decision, because a negative result obtained against a
stated gate is evidence, and because the shape of the failure names what the
platform would need to become predictive.

Three options were considered. Option~(A) was to train on measured generation at
conduit and outfall scale, which is the only training set that would transfer to
the target population. Option~(B) was to train on the labeled fleet actually in
hand (1{,}360 plants combined from EIA-860/923 and the EHA inventory,
1{,}268~EHA and 92~EIA, 250{,}643.66~GWh/yr of measured annual generation) and
transfer that model to outfalls. Option~(C) was to use the same fleet as a
calibration reference only, as Section~4.4.1 does, and build no model. Option~(A)
required labels that had not been found, so the work of this subsystem became the
hunt for them, run against a gate fixed in advance: at least 50 new usable sites
carrying both installed capacity and measured annual energy, or Option~(C) stands.

The hunt covered every source that plausibly carries measured micro-scale
generation. The Bureau of Reclamation RISE service was probed first and holds
water-operations data (canal stage, reservoir release, dissolved gas) with
zero result records against its powerplant-generation parameter and no sub-megawatt
coverage. The FERC databases were downloaded in full: 222 active conduit
exemptions, 1{,}016 active licenses, and the qualifying-conduit notices of intent
created by the Hydropower Regulatory Efficiency Act~\cite{ferc_conduit}. Every one
of them carries authorized capacity and none carries measured generation, which
is structural rather than an oversight: FERC is a permitting system, and operating
generation is reported instead to the Energy Information Administration on Form
EIA-923~\cite{eia923}, which is the source the EHA workbook already
republishes~\cite{hydrosource_eha}. The remaining path was therefore a cross-link
inside data already held. Of 338 canal and conduit plants in the EHA inventory,
115 carry measured annual generation, 80 of them at or below 5~MW and 6 at or
below 1~MW. Cross-referencing by plant code against the 1{,}360 labels already
combined showed that 103 of the 115 were already owned. Eleven were genuinely
new, all of them irrigation canal drops of at least 1~MW, none with head or flow.
Eleven against a gate of fifty is a clear fail.

One site deserves its own paragraph, because it is the closest analogue in the
label universe and because reporting it correctly took two attempts. Point Loma
in San Diego is a 1{,}500~kW machine on a treated-wastewater effluent outfall,
the only true wastewater site among the 115, and its reported net generation is
0~MWh for every year from 2018 through 2022. The first pass selected each plant's
latest non-zero year, which returned 2017 and 2{,}515~MWh and silently concealed
a plant offline for four years; the error was caught in review and the rule
replaced. The rest of the wastewater-adjacent labels are three municipal
drinking-water sites and 111 irrigation canal drops.

The model was killed for four specific reasons, each of which would have to be
removed before it is worth revisiting. The labeled plants have a median installed
capacity of 7{,}700~kW and span 100~kW to 6{,}495~MW, against a target population
whose median viable machine is 3.80~kW. Head and flow are null in 1{,}360 of the
1{,}360 labels, because neither EIA nor EHA collects them, so the architecture's
physics-versus-measured check cannot be run at all: not poorly, but not at
all. The transferable feature intersection between the label set and the scoring
matrix is five columns: latitude, longitude, log installed capacity, a five-band
climate proxy derived from latitude, and state. And the one wastewater data point
is offline.

What survives is the pipeline itself, exercised once as a smoke test to prove the
rails rather than to produce a deliverable. \texttt{src/phase5/train.py} runs
label combination, feature construction, a leakage check, nested cross-validation,
and a final fit on all 1{,}360 rows, using LightGBM~4.6.0~\cite{lightgbm} at
fixed hyper-parameters (400 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, minimum 20
samples per leaf, deterministic mode, seed 0) with no sweep, by design. The
outer loop is five folds stratified by state and the inner loop three folds
grouped by source plant code, so no plant appears on both sides of a split. The
leakage lock is the part worth keeping: \texttt{assert\_no\_leakage} refuses any
feature list containing a column derived from the physics estimate, including
every financial output, the viability flags, and the capacity factor itself, and
it is called both when the training frame is built and again on entry to
cross-validation. Annual revenue is on that denylist because it correlates 0.915
with measured energy, revenue being energy multiplied by a rate. The measured
outcome of that run is reported, with its metrics and its caveats, in
Section~5.1.5.

Three limitations close this subsection. The smoke-test metrics have no product
significance and are labeled as such wherever they appear. The
physics-versus-measured comparison the architecture specified remains un-run and
will remain so until labels carry head and flow. The unblock is a set of
sub-megawatt conduit or outfall installations with measured annual energy, and no
bulk source publishes them; the only path identified is manual collection from
municipal utility reports and WWTP energy audits, one plant at a time. How the
results of the calibrated pipeline are published will now be discussed.

\section{Data Export and Serving Layer}                  % WP T5 — Tom

The export layer is the single boundary between the pipeline and everything
downstream of it: the dashboard of Section~4.6, the figures and tables in this
document, and any reader who wants the underlying numbers. It is responsible for
carrying the full result of Phases~1--4 across that boundary in a form that can be
read without running any of them, and for doing so reproducibly enough that two
people who export the same parquets get the same bytes.

Four options were considered. Option~(A) was a database and a service in front of
it, a PostGIS table behind a query API. Option~(B) was a set of purpose-built JSON
files, one per view, which is what the first implementation did: national totals,
a plant collection for the map, a per-state portfolio file, and a per-plant detail
file. Option~(C) was to ship the parquets to the browser and query them in the
page. Option~(D) was one static GeoJSON feature collection carrying every
property, with every view shape derived on the client. Option~(A) was rejected on
operational cost: a screening artifact that changes only when the pipeline is
re-run does not justify a server, credentials, and an uptime commitment. Option~(B)
was rejected from experience with it: four files computed by four code paths
drift, and every new column means editing all of them. Option~(C) needs a query
engine in the browser and still needs a geometry conversion, so it buys nothing at
this size. Option~(D) won because the frontend then needs no backend at all, the
artifact is a single git-tracked file that can be diffed and hashed, and one file
cannot disagree with itself.

The exporter is \texttt{scripts/export\_geojson.py}. It joins on \texttt{npdes\_id}
with the Phase~4 scorecard as the spine, left-joining Phase~1 for facility name,
city, coordinates, and flow statistics, Phase~2 for the P10, P50, and P90 energy
band and the equivalent-homes conversion, and Phase~3 for gross and net head, both
elevations, head source and confidence, rated flow, and peak efficiency. The result
is 59 properties per feature, grouped as in Table~\ref{tab:geojson_props}: the
original 24 that the first map needed, plus 34 added when the four legacy files
were retired and everything they had computed had to be derivable on the client,
plus the measured-floor energy tier added when the reported calibration band was
re-floored in Section~4.4.1.
Geometry is a point in longitude-latitude order as RFC~7946 requires~\cite{rfc7946},
rounded to five decimals, which is about a meter. Numeric properties are rounded by
class rather than individually: dollars, kilowatt-hours, and counts to whole
numbers, ratios such as internal rate of return and levelized cost to four
decimals, and meters, million-gallons-per-day flows, cubic-meters-per-second
ratings, and efficiencies to one decimal. Any value that is not finite becomes a
JSON null instead of a \texttt{NaN} token, which is not valid JSON. A \texttt{meta}
foreign member, permitted by RFC~7946~\S6.1, carries the number of plants analyzed,
the number scored, and a baseline label, and all three are read from parquet row
counts rather than typed in, so the file cannot claim a provenance its own contents
contradict.

Two files are written into \texttt{exports/} on every default run so that they
cannot drift apart. The first, \texttt{viable\_sites.geojson}, holds the
1{,}138 project-viable sites at 1.90~MB and is the file this document's portfolio
figures are drawn from. The second, \texttt{scored\_sites.geojson}, holds all
3{,}778 scored plants at 6.30~MB
and is what the dashboard loads, so plants that failed the viability gate stay on
the map as evidence rather than disappearing from it. Both carry the same 59
properties on every feature. The write path validates before it returns: the
collection type, the feature count, the coordinate ranges, and the types inside
\texttt{meta}. Features whose coordinates are null or unparseable are dropped and
counted rather than written with a placeholder position; that count is zero in
both files, which it was not before the coordinate guard of Section~4.2.

Determinism was a requirement rather than a happy accident, because a file that
changes when nothing changed cannot be reviewed. The output carries no timestamp
and no run identifier, is written with compact separators, and takes its feature
order from the parquet. Re-running the exporter over the unchanged parquets
reproduces both files byte-identically, at SHA-256 digests beginning
\texttt{ddfc810e} for the viable file and \texttt{b3608816} for the scored file;
the check was last repeated after the fifty-ninth property was added, since any
change to the contract moves both digests.
The practical consequence is that the export is testable in the strongest sense
available: any change to any site's economics changes the digest, and any run that
changed nothing leaves it alone.

Five limitations are named. The dashboard downloads the whole 6.30~MB file before
it draws anything, which is acceptable at 3{,}778 sites and is the wrong shape at
ten times that; vector tiles or a query service is the named fix, and neither was
built. The contract has no version field beyond its baseline label, so renaming a
property breaks consumers silently, and a schema version is the cheapest thing to
add before anyone else builds on it. Infinite payback and levelized cost are
written as a sentinel value of $10^6$, which occurs 823 times in the scored file
and never in the viable file; a consumer that treats it as a number reads a
million-year payback, and the frontend has to guard it explicitly. Regeneration is
manual: nothing in the test suite asserts that the tracked files match the
current parquets, so they can go stale between a pipeline run and a commit.
Finally, the rounding is deliberately lossy, most consequentially at one decimal
place on head, so the exported file is a reporting artifact and any reanalysis
should start from the parquets instead. The visualization system that consumes
this contract will now be discussed.

\section{Frontend Visualization System}                  % WP M1 — Mohamed

The screening pipeline described in the preceding sections of Chapter 4 ends
at a single deterministic data contract, the 59-property GeoJSON file
exported by \texttt{export\_geojson.py}. That file is inert on its own: it
is the frontend's responsibility to turn it into a tool a non-technical
reviewer, an investor, or a municipal utility can actually use to find and
evaluate a candidate site. Concretely, the frontend renders every scored
plant on an interactive national map, lets a user narrow that set by state,
turbine type, payback period, and data-quality tier, and drills from any
site down to a full financial and physical scorecard. It runs entirely as a
static single-page application (SPA): there is no server process, no
database, and no API behind it, a constraint that follows directly from the
export layer's own design and is not an independent decision. How that
constraint shaped the stack, the data layer, and the view architecture will
now be discussed.

\subsection{Stack Selection}

Three architectures were considered for turning the exported GeoJSON into a
usable tool. The first was a server-rendered dashboard, a small Python
backend (Flask or FastAPI) that queries the parquets or the GeoJSON file on
every request and renders HTML server-side. The second was a
notebook-style analytics app, Streamlit or Dash, which trades custom UI
control for fast iteration on data-heavy views. The third was a static
single-page application that fetches one pre-exported file once and does
all filtering, sorting, and view derivation in the browser. The static SPA
was chosen because the pipeline's output is already a batch artifact,
regenerated on demand rather than on every request, so a server that
re-queries it for each page load adds operational surface (a process to
keep running, a host to pay for) without adding any capability the static
file cannot already provide. This also matches how the export layer itself
was designed, byte-deterministic and git-tracked so that the exact file a
browser fetches is the exact file reviewed and committed, with no server-side
state that could drift from it.

Within that choice, three further decisions had to be made. For the
application framework, React 19.2~\cite{react19} was chosen over a
framework-free approach or Vue, because the view set (seven distinct routed
pages sharing a common data layer and a consistent set of formatting and
color-coding rules) is naturally expressed as a small component tree, and
React's ecosystem includes typed, actively maintained bindings for both of
the remaining two decisions. TypeScript~5.7~\cite{typescript} was used
throughout rather than plain JavaScript so that the 59-property data
contract could be declared once, in \texttt{lib/types.ts}, and checked at
build time everywhere it is consumed; a property renamed or dropped in the
exporter surfaces as a compile error in the frontend rather than a silent
\texttt{undefined} on a live dashboard. The build tool is
Vite~7.1~\cite{vite7}, chosen over Create React App or webpack directly for
its native ES module dev server and its \texttt{?url} asset-import syntax,
which is what lets the GeoJSON file be imported as a versioned, hashed
static asset (see Section~4.6.2) instead of being fetched from a hand-built
path.

For the map layer, three libraries were considered: Leaflet with raster
tile layers, Mapbox GL~JS, and MapLibre GL~JS. Leaflet was set aside because
its DOM/SVG-based marker rendering does not scale cleanly to the full
3{,}778-site scored set at national zoom. Mapbox GL was set aside because it
requires a paid API token for both tiles and the GL runtime itself, an
ongoing operating cost and an external dependency this project's
non-technical stakeholders should not have to manage. MapLibre
GL~JS~5.1~\cite{maplibregl}, an open-source, token-free fork of Mapbox GL~JS
v1, was chosen instead: it renders point data as a single WebGL circle layer
regardless of point count, and it is wrapped through
\texttt{react-map-gl}~8.0~\cite{reactmapgl} so the map is expressed
declaratively as \texttt{<Map>}, \texttt{<Source>}, and \texttt{<Layer>}
components rather than through imperative MapLibre calls scattered across
component lifecycle hooks.

For charting, hand-rolled SVG (or a lower-level library such as D3 directly)
and Chart.js were considered against Recharts~3.0~\cite{recharts3}.
Recharts was chosen because its composable, React-native component API
(\texttt{<BarChart>}, \texttt{<ScatterChart>}, \texttt{<AreaChart>}) matches
the rest of the codebase's component style and needed no imperative canvas
management. One chart does not use Recharts: the capacity-factor gauge on
the plant detail page is custom inline SVG, built in \texttt{Gauge.tsx},
because Recharts has no radial-needle gauge primitive, and the display this
project needed, a needle fixed to capacity factor on a 0--100\% scale with
P10/P50/P90 energy figures labeled beneath it, was simpler to draw directly
as an SVG arc than to bend a general-purpose charting library toward it.

\subsection{Data Layer and View Architecture}

The frontend went through two data-layer designs before reaching its
current form. The first, built alongside the initial three-view dashboard,
used a 308-line Python exporter that read the four production parquets and
wrote four separate artifacts under \texttt{frontend/public/data/}: a slim
map GeoJSON, a national KPI summary, one portfolio file per state, and one
detail file per plant. That design required a Python step before every
\texttt{npm run dev}, and it duplicated, in a second file format, exactly
the join logic already implemented in the export layer described in
Section~4.5. It was replaced the same day by the design the frontend uses
today: the single git-tracked \texttt{exports/scored\_sites.geojson}, the
same 59-property, 3{,}778-feature file described in Section~4.5, is the
frontend's only data source.

\texttt{lib/data.ts} implements this as a module-level cached fetch.
\texttt{sitesUrl} is a Vite \texttt{?url} import of the GeoJSON file one
directory above the frontend package, so the exact file a build embeds is
the exact file tracked in the repository, with no copy step and no
possibility of the two drifting. The first call to \texttt{loadSites()}
issues one \texttt{fetch()} and stores the pending promise; every
subsequent call across every view returns that same cached promise rather
than re-fetching, so navigating between the seven routes costs one network
request for the life of the session. A local \texttt{SiteProps} interface
mirrors the exporter's 59 properties field for field, and four exported
functions, \texttt{fetchPlants}, \texttt{fetchNational},
\texttt{fetchPortfolio(state)}, and \texttt{fetchPlant(id)}, derive the four
view-specific shapes the components consume entirely in the browser. This
client-side derivation reproduces classification rules that previously
lived in the Python exporter: \texttt{payback()} treats any value at or
above $10^5$ as the pipeline's ``never pays back'' sentinel and rounds the
remainder to two decimal places \emph{before} banding, because the export
layer's own rounding happens before its banding and a boundary value such
as 4.9996~yr bands as ``high'' (\textless 5~yr) on the raw figure but
``moderate'' on the rounded one; \texttt{viabilityBand()} then bins that
rounded payback into the four legend categories shown on every map and
table (high \textless 5~yr, moderate 5--15~yr, marginal 15--20~yr,
non-viable otherwise); and \texttt{siteConfidence()} maps the exporter's
\texttt{data\_quality} and \texttt{project\_viable\_high\_confidence}
fields onto a three-level High/Medium/Lower badge shown throughout the
interface.

Seven routes are declared in \texttt{App.tsx} using React Router~7. The
root route, \texttt{NationalMap}, is the landing view: a MapLibre map of
every scored site colored by viability band, a left filter panel (state,
turbine type including an explicit ``Unassigned'' bucket, a 1--20~yr payback
slider where the maximum setting removes the payback filter entirely rather
than clamping at 20, and a high-confidence-only toggle), header KPI tiles
recomputed live from the currently filtered feature set rather than from a
static national total, and a right-hand panel listing the top five
opportunities by NPV plus a bar chart of viable sites by state.
\texttt{/state/:code} (\texttt{StatePortfolio}) shows every scored site in
one state as a sortable, paginated, CSV-exportable table alongside an
energy bar chart of the top ten sites and a payback-versus-NPV risk/return
scatter; Figure~\ref{fig:m1-stateportfolio} shows the California portfolio.
\texttt{/plant/:id} (\texttt{PlantDetail}) is the single-site
view: a three-column layout presenting flow data, elevation data, and a
satellite mini-map on the left; the energy-recovery gauge and a recommended
turbine card with a synthesized efficiency curve in the center; and a
financial scorecard, a sensitivity tornado chart, and outbound
next-step links (a per-turbine-type manufacturer page, the DOE Water Power
Technologies Office funding page, and a link to similar sites filtered by
the same state and turbine type) on the right. \texttt{/opportunities} and
\texttt{/plants} both reuse a single shared \texttt{SiteTable} component,
pre-filtered to \texttt{project\_viable} sites in the first case and
unfiltered across all 3{,}778 scored sites in the second, each with its own
URL-parameter filters so either can serve as a deep-link target from
another view. \texttt{/analytics}, in Figure~\ref{fig:m1-analytics}, aggregates the whole
portfolio into a payback-distribution histogram, a per-state bar, a
turbine-mix bar, and a combined viability-band and confidence-tier
breakdown. \texttt{/reports}
presents the national summary as a plain key-value grid alongside one-click

\subsection{Interaction, Filtering, and Export}

Two shared conventions run across every filterable view. First, filter
state lives in the URL query string through React Router's
\texttt{useSearchParams}, not in component state: \texttt{NationalMap}
encodes state, turbine-type list, maximum payback, and the high-confidence
toggle as \texttt{?state=\&turb=\&pb=\&hc=}, and \texttt{Plants} does the
same for its own filter set. A filtered view is therefore a shareable,
bookmarkable, refresh-safe link rather than transient client state, and it
is also how one view deep-links into another, for example the ``View
Similar Sites'' action on the plant detail page, which links to
\texttt{/plants} pre-filtered to the same state and turbine type. Second,
every table (\texttt{SiteTable}, used by both \texttt{Opportunities} and
\texttt{Plants}, and the portfolio table in \texttt{StatePortfolio}) is
independently sortable by column, searchable by name, city, state, or
NPDES~ID, paginated at 50 rows per page, and exportable to CSV through a
shared \texttt{downloadCsv} helper that builds the file as a
\texttt{Blob} in the browser and triggers a download with no server round
trip.

On the map itself, clicking any point navigates to that site's detail
page; hovering shows a popup with name, location, energy, payback, and
NPV. Selecting a state from the filter panel calls MapLibre's
\texttt{fitBounds} against the minimum and maximum longitude and latitude
of that state's features, easing the view to it; this call is gated on the
map's \texttt{onLoad} event, because an earlier version fired
\texttt{fitBounds} before the underlying map instance existed whenever a
state was present in the URL on first page load, a race that silently
failed to zoom on a fresh deep link. A basemap toggle switches between two
token-free raster sources, CARTO Positron for a light reference map and
Esri World Imagery for satellite, drawn as a two-button control over the
map canvas; the same satellite style is reused, at a fixed close zoom, for
the small locator map on the plant detail page.

\subsection{Limitations}

Five limitations are named. First, the initial page load fetches the
entire 6.30~MB scored-sites file regardless of which view is opened, the
same whole-file cost noted from the export side in Section~4.5; this is
acceptable at 3{,}778 sites but would need a change, most likely a
tiled or paginated data endpoint, if the screened fleet grew by an order of
magnitude. Second, the production JavaScript bundle is not code-split by
route, so the MapLibre GL runtime is downloaded even when a user opens
\texttt{/reports} and never touches the map; route-level lazy loading
(React's \texttt{lazy()}) would defer that cost to the views that need it
and is the named fix, not yet built. Third, the national map draws every
point individually with no clustering, so dense regions such as the
Northeast corridor overplot at the default national zoom; the per-state
zoom-to-bounds interaction mitigates this once a state is selected but does
not address the initial national view, and marker clustering at low zoom is
the deferred fix. Fourth, every filter, sort, search, and pagination
operation runs client-side over the full in-memory feature array; this
costs nothing perceptible at 3{,}778 features but is a ceiling that would
require server-side query support to lift. Fifth, the efficiency curve
drawn on the plant detail page is a synthesized unimodal approximation
centered on each turbine's rated flow and peak efficiency, not a measured
or vendor-published per-unit curve; it exists to make the recommended
turbine's operating envelope legible at a glance and should not be read as
an engineering specification. A sixth item is procedural rather than
architectural and is worth recording here: the manufacturer links surfaced
on the plant detail page (one vendor per recommended turbine type) are
representative suppliers chosen for the demonstration, not endorsements or
confirmed live quotes, and should be re-verified before any external use of
the dashboard. The frontend's measured build and quality-assurance results
are reported next, in Section~5.1.6.

This chapter reports what the platform produced when run end to end on the
re-baselined national dataset of 6~July 2026. One distinction governs every number
in it. The measured inputs are the discharge records and the elevations: monthly
flows reported by permit holders to the Environmental Protection
Agency~\cite{epa_echo} and point elevations returned by the 3DEP query
service~\cite{usgs_3dep}. Everything downstream (head, machine choice,
efficiency, energy, cost, and cash flow) is modeled, and no WOWERS site has
been built or metered. Results are therefore reported as a screen with stated
bounds, and wherever a modeled figure differs from what a stated assumption
would give, the gap is quantified rather than absorbed.

\subsection{Best Case and Expected System Performance}   % WP T6 — Tom

The best case is the pipeline's own output under its own assumptions, and it is
established here before any calibrated figure is presented. Table~\ref{tab:ideal}
gives it for the 1{,}138 project-viable sites, split by the permitting tier of

median efficiency, and mean flow, while this one divides by the rating the turbine
selector chose, and a rating set at design flow is above mean-flow power at most
plants. Both are correct against their own denominators and neither is measured.

\subsection{Energy and Financial Results}                % WP T6 — Tom

The viable portfolio is small-plant heavy and top-heavy in energy at the same
time. Its median site is rated at 12.96~kW and makes 93{,}350~kWh/yr, but the ten
largest sites hold 18.9~\% of the portfolio's energy, the largest hundred hold
60.4~\%, and the largest 250 hold 80.4~\%. Read against the permitting tiers of
Table~\ref{tab:ideal}, 42 plants above 250~kW carry 43.7~\% of the energy and
61.0~\% of the net present value while making up 3.7~\% of the site count. Any
commercial reading of this portfolio has to account for that shape: the tail
justifies the screen, and the head pays for it.

Per-site economics at the median are a payback of 9.83~yr, a real internal rate of
return of 9.40~\%, a levelized cost of \$0.0683/kWh, capital cost of \$66{,}421,
and net present value of \$29{,}693. Capital intensity is high for the scale:
\$3{,}676/kW of equipment at the median and \$5{,}577/kW all-in, the difference
being the installation, interconnection, and permitting lines that do not shrink
with machine size. Generation is a trim rather than a supply: the median viable
site offsets 2.016~\% of its own plant's estimated electricity use.

The gradient across the scored fleet matters more than the binary gate, and all
3{,}778 sites carry it. On payback, 94 sites are at or below 5~yr and hold
181.7~GWh/yr, 507 fall between 5 and 10~yr with 153.9~GWh/yr, 537 between 10 and
20~yr with 73.6~GWh/yr, and 2{,}640 exceed 20~yr or never pay back. On net present
value, 103 sites clear \$500k and hold 241.1~GWh/yr while 879 sit between zero and
\$100k with 95.6~GWh/yr. On rate of return, 195 sites exceed 15~\% and hold
242.0~GWh/yr, and 823 have no real solution at all. The three gradients are
computed independently and do not partition the fleet the same way, which is
deliberate: a site that is marginal on payback and strong on rate of return is a
different commercial proposition from one that fails both, and a single flag would
hide that.

Two properties of the viable cohort qualify all of the above. First, 848 of the
1{,}138 sites, 74.5~\%, carry a measured discharge-monitoring flow duration curve;
the remaining 290 rest on a design-flow or annual-average figure and are flagged
in the export as lower confidence. That exposure is not proportional to the
count: the 290 carry \$193.6M of the \$310.1M portfolio net present value,
62.4~\%, because the design-flow and annual-average fallbacks include many of
the largest plants, and the 22 such sites above 250~kW hold \$139.2M on their
own. The 848 measured-curve sites carry the remaining \$116.5M, which is the
base figure of every sensitivity sweep in Section~4.3.6. Second, 778 sites carry an elevation-derived
head and 360 still carry the Phase 2 archetype, and those 360 hold 110.29~GWh/yr,
27.0~\% of the portfolio energy. A quarter of the headline therefore rests on the
assumption Section~4.3.4 exists to remove, which is the honest reading of the
number and the first thing a site visit would test.

\subsection{Capacity-Factor Calibration}                 % WP T6 — Tom

Section~4.4.1 set out the method; this subsection reports what it does to the
result. Benchmarked against 629 real small-hydro plants over 9{,}798 plant-years,
the portfolio's implied capacity factor of 0.8725 sits above the measured
distribution at every percentile: the river-hydro median is 0.3902 and even its
seventy-fifth percentile, 0.5409, is below the WOWERS tenth percentile of 0.8556.
Substituting measured capacity factors gives the band of Table~\ref{tab:cfband}:
89.8~GWh/yr at the metered Point Loma capacity factor, 114.4 across 115 metered
conduit plants, 118.9 at the river-hydro first quartile, 183.0 at its median, and
281.4~GWh/yr at the Conduit~3-anchored optimistic figure of 0.60, against the
409.2~GWh/yr ceiling. The reported band is 89.8--281~GWh/yr. Its floor is the
metered treated-wastewater figure; the two tiers immediately above it, drawn from
datasets that share no plants with each other, land within 4.5~GWh/yr of one another and
within 30~GWh/yr of the floor, so the low end of the band is robust to which defensible
choice is made while the upper end rests on a single design projection.

That is a 61.4~\% fall in viable site count against a 31.2~\% fall in energy at
the optimistic tier, and the mechanism is the fixed cost structure named in
Section~5.1.1: a plant whose capital cost is dominated by interconnection and
permitting has no way to absorb a one-third revenue cut. The gap is acceptable for
a screening deliverable, because the purpose of the screen is to rank and bound
rather than to underwrite, and every site retains its full scorecard so a
different assumption can be applied to it. It is not acceptable for a site
proposal. Closing it needs the thing this work could not obtain: measured annual
generation from an operating wastewater-outfall turbine, which would replace the
substituted capacity factor with an observed one. Until then the honest headline
is the band and not its upper end, and the defensible planning figure is the
floor rather than the optimistic tier: the floor is the only part of the band
that two independent metered datasets agree on, while the upper tier rests on a
single project's design projection. A reader who needs one number for planning
should take 89.8--118.9~GWh/yr and treat 281.4~GWh/yr as the ceiling of
plausibility rather than the expectation.

\subsection{Machine-Learning Feasibility (Negative Result)} % WP T6 — Tom

The learned correction described in Section~4.4.2 was tested against a gate fixed
before the search began (at least 50 new labeled sites carrying installed
capacity and measured annual energy) and the search returned 11. The gate
failed and the model was killed as a product deliverable. Reporting that outcome
with its numbers is the point of this subsection.

What was run afterwards is a smoke test whose only purpose is to prove the code
path, and its metrics have no product significance. It trains LightGBM on the
1{,}360 combined EIA and EHA dam labels using five features, with five outer folds
stratified by state and three inner folds grouped by plant, and compares against
two baselines evaluated on identical splits: a predict-the-training-mean model,
and a capacity-factor model that predicts capacity times 8{,}760 times the
training fold's median capacity factor. Results are in Table~\ref{tab:smoke}.

transferable finding in the chapter. Micro-hydropower at wastewater outfalls does
not lack modeling capacity; it lacks measured plants. Until sub-megawatt conduit
installations report head, flow, and annual energy in a public dataset, a learned
model of this asset class cannot be trained, evaluated, or falsified.

\textbf{Discrepancy: bundle size and deferred code-splitting.} The
measured result departs from the ideal one in a specific, bounded way.
The gap: two chunks, the 711.33~kB application bundle and the
1{,}053.37~kB MapLibre runtime, are downloaded on every route, including
\texttt{/reports}, which never renders a map, for a combined 1{,}764.7~kB

page. The mechanism: \texttt{App.tsx} imports all seven views statically,
and \texttt{MapView} is pulled in transitively through
\texttt{NationalMap} and through \texttt{PlantDetail}'s mini-map, so the
bundler has no route boundary at which it could separate the MapLibre
dependency into its own lazily loaded chunk. Whether this is acceptable
for the current scope: yes. At 499.46~kB gzipped total and a demonstration
audience of one reviewer at a time rather than concurrent production
traffic, the cost is a fraction of a second on any real connection, and it
was accepted as a known cost rather than an oversight in the
2026-07-07 session that first flagged it. The fix, not yet built, is
route-level code-splitting with React's \texttt{lazy()} so that the
MapLibre chunk is requested only when \texttt{NationalMap} or
\texttt{PlantDetail} is the active route. Map clustering at low zoom (see
the limitation named in Section~4.6.4) and a live backend to replace the
static GeoJSON fetch remain separately deferred and are not measured
here, because neither has been built in any form to measure.

\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}

We designed and built a national screening platform for micro-hydropower recovery
at wastewater outfalls, and tested it end to end on the full United States fleet.
The system ingests federal discharge records and national elevation data, converts
them into a flow duration curve and a net head for every plant it can, samples an
energy estimate with an explicit uncertainty band, selects a machine from a vendor
catalogue, builds a thirty-year financial scorecard, calibrates the energy result
against measured capacity factors from real operating plants, and publishes the
outcome as a single deterministic data contract that a static web application
renders as an interactive national map. Measured against the minimum viable
product WOWERS would need, that fulfills the screening requirement, the ranking
requirement, the bounded-estimate requirement, and the delivery requirement. It
does not fulfill the validation requirement, and Chapter~5 is explicit about why.

The demonstration screened 17{,}148 publicly owned treatment works and carried
5{,}464 of them through the energy model, 4{,}860 through head estimation, and
3{,}778 through turbine selection to a full financial scorecard, of which 1{,}138
clear a viability gate of positive net present value, payback within twenty years,
and a real internal rate of return. Those 1{,}138 sites represent 58.59~MW of
rated capacity and 409.17~GWh/yr under the model's own assumptions, against
\$211.33M of capital cost, \$41.23M/yr of revenue, a portfolio net present value
of \$310.13M, and a median payback of 9.83~yr. Benchmarked against 629 real
small-hydro plants over 9{,}798 plant-years, the same portfolio bounds to a
measured floor of 89.8~GWh/yr from the only metered treated-wastewater conduit plant in
the country, corroborated from above at 114.4~GWh/yr by 115 metered conduit plants and at
118.9~GWh/yr by the river-hydro first quartile, and an optimistic upper scenario of
281.4~GWh/yr resting on one project's design projection; re-scoring every site
through the cash-flow model at that upper capacity factor leaves 439 sites viable
and \$152.64M of net present
value. The headline this work stands behind is the band and not its upper end.

Three themes would carry the platform from a screen to a product: replacing
modeled head with measured head, turning the delivery layer from a static file
into a service, and validating the whole chain against a real installation.

The first theme is the one that matters most, because head is both the input the
model cannot measure and the input the answer depends on most. Across the scored
fleet, net head is the dominant sensitivity at 2{,}803 of 3{,}778 sites, and
halving it turns the portfolio net present value negative. The elevation
difference the platform uses has a published vertical accuracy of 2.44~m RMSE at
each of the two points it differences, while the median accepted head is 4.144~m,
and 360 project-viable sites carry no elevation-derived head at all; they still
run on a literature archetype and hold 27.0~\% of the portfolio energy. Three
specific changes would close this. Snapping each outfall coordinate onto the
nearest National Hydrography Dataset flowline would fix the 1{,}203 sites whose
outfall currently resolves above their plant, which is physically impossible for a
gravity outfall. Computing a per-site head error bound, rather than the fleet-level
argument this work gives, would let each site carry an honest interval instead of
a point. And surveying a sample of outfall inverts would establish whether the
elevation proxy is biased or merely noisy, which is currently unknown.

The second theme is the delivery layer. The dashboard reads a single 6.30~MB
GeoJSON file and derives every view from it in the browser, which is the right
architecture at 3{,}778 sites and the wrong one an order of magnitude above that.
The specific work is to split the client bundle so the mapping library loads
independently of the application shell, to cluster map markers so dense regions
stay legible and responsive, and to replace the static file with a query service
so a client fetches the state it is looking at rather than the whole country. The
data contract should also carry a schema version, which it currently does not, so
that a renamed property fails loudly at the consumer rather than silently.

The third theme is validation, and it is the one that requires leaving the desk.
No WOWERS site has been instrumented, and the machine-learning path that would
have learned a correction from measured generation was abandoned because the
labels do not exist: an exhaustive search of federal sources returned eleven new
conduit-scale plants against a threshold of fifty, none of them wastewater, and
the single true wastewater installation in the national inventory has been offline
since 2018. That is a data-availability finding rather than a modeling failure,
and only a field program resolves it. A single pilot (one instrumented
outfall at a plant from the shortlist, reporting head, flow, and generation for a
year) would convert the calibration band from a substituted assumption into a
measurement, and would be worth more to this platform than any further modeling
work.

What this work delivers is the first step toward customer value rather than the
finished service: a defensible, reproducible, national answer to the question of
which wastewater treatment plants are worth a feasibility study, delivered at a
marginal cost per site of approximately zero, with its uncertainty stated in the
open. The screen is the part of WOWERS that had to be de-risked first, because
everything the venture proposes to sell depends on it being credible. It now is,
within bounds this document names precisely.

Two features of that figure are worth naming. The band is uniform in relative
width: the ninetieth percentile is between 2.24 and 2.47 times the tenth at every
plant in the fleet, with a median of 2.276. That uniformity is not evidence of a
well-behaved estimate; it is the signature of an assumption. The width is set
almost entirely by the triangular head distribution of
Table~\ref{tab:head_archetypes}, which takes the same three shapes across all
5{,}464 plants, so the histogram separates into exactly three clusters, one per
archetype, with the small-plant archetype the widest at 2.409. The flow duration
curve, the only measured input to the sampler, contributes almost none of the
spread. The correct reading is that the P10--P90 band quantifies the head
assumption rather than the uncertainty of the estimate, and that a plant's true
energy can sit outside it whenever the assumed head is wrong, which
Section~5.1.2 shows it frequently is, since replacing the archetype with a measured
elevation difference moved fleet energy by 104.44~GWh/yr.

median project-viable qualified-conduit site returns 72~MWh/yr against 565~MWh/yr under

portfolio construction is that site count and energy pull in opposite directions. The

its energy, while the 42 full-NEPA sites are 3.7~\% by count and 43.7~\% of the energy.

same caveat.
